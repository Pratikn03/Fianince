 Feedback Stability, Auditable Memory, and Financial Constraints

                                              Abstract

      When algorithmic learning turns price formation into a closed loop, can observable
      market data detect destabilizing feedback? We establish that public price tapes identify
      only merged equivalence classes; resolving near-coincident timescales requires probe
      budgets scaling as Ω(δ −4 ), forcing mandatory surveillance abstention. Order books
      exhibit subcritical baseline feedback; under liquidity depletion, residual volatility surges
      62.7× while the price-impact slope rises modestly. An exchange fee overhaul reveals
      no detectable feedback response: zero fees sparked a 180% volume surge while leaving
      persistence unchanged. Finally, spectral stability does not ensure dealer solvency: non-
      normal amplification contracts safe radii, exhausting capital inside the stable regime.


Keywords: Adaptive learning, algorithmic trading, financial stability, market microstruc-
ture, system identification, non-normal dynamics, Kreiss constant.

1     Introduction
The delegation of financial execution and market making to autonomous algorithms has fun-
damentally transformed the microstructural physics of modern electronic exchanges. Over
the past three decades, financial markets have undergone an unprecedented structural tran-
sition from human-intermediated floor trading and manual specialist posts (such as the
historic open-outcry pits of Chicago and the specialist trading posts of the New York Stock
Exchange) to fully automated, electronic continuous double auctions housed in co-located
server racks in Secaucus, New Jersey (Equinix NY4) and Slough, United Kingdom (Equinix
LD4). In contemporary equity, sovereign debt, foreign exchange, and digital asset venues,
automated execution engines, statistical arbitrageurs, and high-frequency market makers
(HFMs) submit and cancel the overwhelming majority of limit quotes, respond to order
flow in microseconds, and continuously adapt their execution strategies to prevailing mar-
ket conditions (Hasbrouck, 1991; Menkveld, 2013; Budish, Cramton, and Shim, 2015; Biais,
Foucault, and Moinas, 2015).
     When trading algorithms condition their order submission and quote-placement decisions
on recent transaction prices, prevailing order book imbalances, and trade innovations, the
price-formation process ceases to be an open-loop mechanism driven purely by exogenous
informational updates regarding fundamentals. Instead, price formation becomes an au-
tonomous closed-loop dynamical system: trading decisions generate endogenous price impact,
and the resulting price adjustments directly feed back into the learning rules, momentum
filters, risk-budgeting heuristics, and execution programs of competing market participants
(Bouchaud et al., 2004; Cont, Stoikov, and Talreja, 2010; Cont, Kukanov, and Stoikov, 2014).
     This closed-loop structural reality was dramatically underscored by major episodic dis-
ruptions in electronic markets over the past decade and a half. During the May 6, 2010 Flash
Crash, the E-mini S&P 500 index collapsed by more than 9% in a matter of minutes before
rebounding, triggered when an automated selling program interacted with high-frequency
market makers whose internal risk management algorithms rapidly depleted resting order
book depth. Similar self-amplifying cascades occurred during the October 15, 2014 Treasury
flash rally, the August 24, 2015 equity exchange-traded fund (ETF) pricing breakdowns, and
the catastrophic liquidity unpegs across digital asset exchanges in March 2020 and November
2022. In each of these events, markets populated by sophisticated algorithms designed to
optimize execution individually exhibited severe collective fragility.
     These structural phenomena confront financial economists, central banks, and market
regulators with an urgent, foundational question:

       Can observable market data identify destabilizing adaptive feedback, when does

    In this paper, we address this question directly by integrating realization-theoretic sys-
tem identification with empirical limit order book econometrics and balance-sheet financial
constraints. Rather than making speculative assertions regarding black-box artificial intel-
ligence, we organize our theoretical and empirical findings around three headline contri-
butions:

Contribution 1: Fundamental Identification Boundaries and Mandatory Absten-
tion. We establish what public price and order-flow data can and cannot reveal about
underlying learning algorithms. We prove that in any linear market–learning system with
heterogeneous adaptive cohorts, aggregate public price responses identify only the merged
rate–weight representation. Individual algorithmic cohorts that share common adaptation
rates cannot be distinguished from public price feeds—an exact realization-theoretic equiva-
lence class (Theorem 2.3). Furthermore, detecting heterogeneity between two cohorts whose
adaptation timescales differ by a small margin δ requires an active probing budget grow-
ing at the severe sample-complexity rate of Ω(δ −4 ) (Theorem 2.3). With finite observation
windows, any error-controlled stability classifier must strictly abstain from issuing safety
certificates near the critical stability boundary (Theorem 2.5). These limits are structural
properties of linear dynamical systems, not artifacts of statistical noise.

Contribution 2: Empirical Market Evidence Across Regimes and Fee-Shock
Response. Using continuous high-frequency Level-3 limit order books and a structured
multi-year panel of 768 asset-days on Binance Spot (2021–2024), we estimate a model-
derived reduced-form feedback parameter Q̂ and closed-loop persistence Ŝ = â + Q̂. In
normal conditions, baseline feedback is consistently small, positive, and strictly subcritical
(Q̂ = 0.0082, Ŝ = 0.921 < 1.0); the reduced-form estimate is numerically similar under
minimum-distance covariance matching (QMD = 0.0086), although the pooled overidentify-
ing restrictions reject the parsimonious linear specification (J = 73.0, df = 4, p < 0.001),
while Rigobon heteroskedasticity identification reveals negative contemporaneous buffering
(QRig = −0.01913). However, using Threshold Vector Autoregressions and Markov-switching
models on Level-3 order books, we find that liquidity stress is sharply state-dependent: dur-
ing liquidity depletion, residual volatility explodes by 62.7× (p < 10−300 ) and resting depth
collapses, while the price-impact slope differential is modest and statistically insignificant
(∆q̂ = +0.0694, t = 0.94, p = 0.35).

1.1    Related Literature
Our work bridges four major strands of literature across financial economics, market mi-
crostructure, systemic risk, and dynamical systems theory.

High-Frequency Trading and Market Microstructure. Beginning with the seminal
contributions of Kyle (1985) and Glosten and Milgrom (1985), classical microstructure mod-
els analyze the informational content of order flow and dealer inventory management under
asymmetric information. Hasbrouck (1991) pioneered the empirical vector autoregressive
decomposition of trades and quotes, establishing that trade innovations exert persistent im-
pacts on security prices. Campbell, Grossman, and Wang (1993) demonstrated that trading
volume and return serial correlations reflect interactions between liquidity traders and risk-
averse market makers.
    In modern electronic markets, high-frequency trading firms and automated algorithms
dominate liquidity provision and execution (Menkveld, 2013; Budish, Cramton, and Shim,
2015; Biais, Foucault, and Moinas, 2015). While Hendershott, Jones, and Menkveld (2011)
document that algorithmic trading improves liquidity and narrows bid-ask spreads during
normal trading periods, Budish, Cramton, and Shim (2015) show that continuous double
auctions generate socially wasteful latency arms races, and Biais, Foucault, and Moinas
(2015) demonstrate that algorithmic speed advantages can lead to adverse selection spirals.
We advance this literature by formalizing the closed loop between algorithmic learning rules
and endogenous price impact, establishing fundamental realization-theoretic bounds on what
econometricians can infer from public ticker feeds.

Price Impact and Limit Order Book Dynamics. A extensive literature examines the
physics of price impact in electronic limit order books (Almgren and Chriss, 2000; Bouchaud
et al., 2004; Cont, Stoikov, and Talreja, 2010; Cont, Kukanov, and Stoikov, 2014). Empirical
studies consistently reveal that signed order flow exhibits strong positive autocorrelation over
hundreds of trades (long memory), while price returns remain approximately unpredictable
(Bouchaud et al., 2004; Cont, Kukanov, and Stoikov, 2014). This apparent paradox is
resolved by subtle negative feedback in quote replacement and cancellation. Cont, Kukanov,
and Stoikov (2014) introduce Order Flow Imbalance (OFI) as a powerful predictor of short-
term price movements across multiple depth levels. We explicitly incorporate and control for
order-splitting memory in our econometric models, demonstrating that even after purging 30
to 90 lags of signed flow memory, a positive and statistically significant feedback component
persists in continuous high-frequency trading.

Intermediary Solvency, Funding Liquidity, and Systemic Risk. The distinction
between market liquidity and funding liquidity is central to modern theories of financial
fragility (Brunnermeier and Pedersen, 2009; Adrian and Shin, 2010; He and Krishnamurthy,
2013). When leveraged market makers suffer balance-sheet drawdowns, risk-management
constraints (such as Value-at-Risk limits or regulatory margin requirements) force them to
withdraw quotes, widening spreads and amplifying price dislocations (Greenwood, Landier,
and Thesmar, 2015; Eisenberg and Noe, 2001). Our analysis demonstrates that non-normal
transient amplification can trigger intermediary insolvency even when the underlying price-
formation process is spectrally stable (ρ(J) < 1).

Non-Normal Dynamics and the Kreiss Matrix Theorem. In applied mathematics
and fluid mechanics, non-normal linear systems have long been recognized as capable of
explosive transient growth despite asymptotic eigenvalue decay (Kreiss, 1962; Spijker, 1991;
Trefethen and Embree, 2005). While eigenvalues govern asymptotic behavior as t → ∞,
the pseudospectrum and the Kreiss constant determine short-term amplification. Although
non-normal operator theory has been applied in macroeconomics to examine input-output
networks, its application to high-frequency market microstructure and dealer solvency is
novel. We derive the exact safe shock radius for leveraged dealers, prove tight bilateral
bounds using Spijker’s optimal constant, and demonstrate why spectral stability alone cannot
guarantee intermediary solvency.

1.2    The 10-Study Empirical Program
Rather than presenting isolated empirical exercises, our empirical program is organized as
a unified, ten-module chain where each study systematically addresses an econometric or
economic objection raised by the preceding one:
  1. Baseline Crypto Panel: Measures baseline feedback Q̂ and persistence Ŝ across
     768 asset-days on Binance Spot, establishing subcritical positive feedback in normal
     conditions.
  2. Regulated Equity Replication: Replicates the baseline model on NASDAQ Nordic
     FI-2010 limit order books (272,941 snapshots across 5 stocks), replicating the qualita-
     tive subcritical pattern in regulated equities.
  3. Cross-Venue Propagation: Examines simultaneous 1-second order books across
     Coinbase and Binance, evaluating whether feedback is local or propagates across ex-
     changes.
  4. Liquidity-State Dependence: Estimates Threshold VAR and Hamilton regime-
     switching models on Level-3 BTC books, showing that liquidity depletion produces a
     large volatility and effective price-sensitivity regime shift.
  5. Flagship Natural Experiment: Exploits the July 8, 2022 Binance zero-fee trading
     schedule shock via Difference-in-Differences, finding no detectable feedback response
     despite a massive volume surge.
  6. Dynamic Event Study: Verifies parallel pre-trends, dynamic treatment persistence,
     and cross-sectional permutation inference for the natural experiment.
  7. Agent Attribution Limits: Proves theoretically and empirically that public order
     flows cannot identify individual algorithmic cohorts without regulatory audit trails.
  8. Financial Consequence Test: Evaluates dealer inventory buffer exhaustion and
     capital solvency, demonstrating that inventory buffer halts jump to 69.1% under con-
     strained capacity and solvency failure reaches 100% under high persistence.
  9. Falsification and Placebo Battery: Tests order-splitting controls up to 90 lags,
     day-shuffled placebos, lead order flow, and completely monotone memory cones.
 10. Documented Out-of-Sample Holdout: Evaluates the frozen out-of-sample econo-
     metric pipeline on untouched January 2023 Bitcoin perpetual futures data (935,199
     records).

2     Theoretical Model and Identification Boundaries
2.1    Microfoundations of Closed-Loop Price Formation
We formalize an electronic financial market sampled at discrete uniform time intervals
t ∈ {0, 1, 2, . . . }, where time step ∆ represents the operational sampling frequency (e.g.,
1 second). Let pt denote the log mid-price of a traded asset, and let p∗t denote the unob-
served fundamental value, which evolves as a martingale:

                          p∗t+1 = p∗t + ξt+1 ,       ξt+1 ∼ i.i.d.(0, σξ2 ).                    (3)

We define the pricing error (displacement from fundamental) as xt ≡ pt − p∗t .

Dealer Pricing and Kyle Auction Microfoundation. Following the continuous auc-
tion framework of Kyle (1985) adapted to discrete quote adjustments (Hasbrouck, 1991;
Bouchaud et al., 2004), a competitive, risk-neutral market maker sets quotes to absorb ag-
gregate order flow. Total incoming order flow at time t consists of two components: aggregate
demand ut submitted by algorithmic trading agents, and exogenous liquidity/hedging noise
εt+1 ∼ i.i.d.(0, σε2 ). In addition, fundamentalist arbitrageurs submit mean-reverting demand
−φxt whenever prices deviate from fundamentals.

The Non-Normal Market Jacobian. Consider the closed-loop Jacobian matrix J from
(14). In financial markets, price impact and memory adaptation are asymmetric: order
flow directly affects prices through contemporaneous impact κ, while price innovations affect
memory through learning rate η. As a result, JJ ⊤ − J ⊤ J ̸= 0: the closed-loop market Jaco-

inside the unit circle (|λi (J)| ≤ ρ(J) < 1), matrix powers J t can undergo massive transient
amplification before decaying asymptotically as t → ∞ (Trefethen and Embree, 2005).

6.3    Falsification and Placebo Battery
To verify that our baseline feedback estimates (Q̂ > 0) reflect genuine economic responsive-
ness rather than econometric artifacts, we execute a comprehensive battery of falsification
checks:
   1. Order-Splitting Sign Memory Controls: Large parent orders split into small
      child orders induce long memory in trade signs (γ ∈ [0.22, 0.35]). Following (47),
      we compute memory-purged feedback Q̂L controlling for up to L = 90 lags of signed
      flow. Controlling for 30 lags of order splitting (Q̂30 ) reduces feedback by 20–30%, but
      leaves positive feedback statistically significant across all assets (Q̂30 = 0.0112 for BTC,
      p < 10−15 ; Figure 3(b)).
   2. Day-Shuffled Placebos: Pairing return series xt from day d with order flow ft from
      day d+1 yields Q̂placebo = 0.0000±0.0001 (t = 0.03), confirming zero spurious feedback
      under temporal randomization.
   3. Lead Order-Flow Placebos: Regressing current returns on future order flow ft+1
      yields zero predictive power (t = −0.12, p = 0.904), providing no evidence of a reverse-
      timing artifact.
   4. Completely Monotone Cone Test: Evaluating the empirical memory kernel against
      the completely monotone cone formally rejects purely contrarian execution models
      (p < 0.001).

7     Regulatory Policy Implications and Conclusion
7.1    Pre-Trade Surveillance vs. Post-Trade Circuit Breakers
Our theoretical and empirical findings carry direct implications for modern financial market
surveillance, notably SEC Rule 15c3-5 (Market Access Rule) in the United States and MiFID
II Regulatory Technical Standard 6 (RTS 6) in the European Union. Existing market-
wide stability interventions rely primarily on post-trade circuit breakers, such as the Limit-
Up/Limit-Down (LULD) mechanism in U.S. equities. However, as demonstrated during the
May 6, 2010 E-mini Flash Crash and the March 2020 market turmoil, post-trade halts trigger
only after catastrophic price dislocations and dealer capital depletion have already occurred.
Within the model, the safe-radius result motivates a capital-aware pre-trade gating rule:
exchanges dynamically pace maximum allowable order submission rates based on prevailing
depth and dealer capital, bounding aggressive flow before execution to mitigate dealer buffer
exhaustion while preserving continuous price discovery during calm trading.

7.3    Market Design: Continuous Auctions vs. Frequent Batch Auc-
       tions
Our findings also speak directly to the structural debate on market design initiated by Bud-
ish, Cramton, and Shim (2015), who propose replacing continuous double auctions with
Frequent Batch Auctions (FBAs) to eliminate latency arbitrage. While FBAs eliminate
microsecond sniper races within batch intervals, they do not resolve closed-loop adaptive
feedback across batches: algorithmic momentum strategies updating across consecutive 100-
millisecond auction clearings still generate price impact loops. Kreiss-bounded rate gat-
ing complements batching by providing a continuous, state-dependent model-based capital
bound that operates effectively under both continuous matching and discrete batch auctions.


7.4    Information Missing from Public Market Feeds
Our realization-theoretic theorems prove that public market data suffer from fundamen-
tal blind spots: (i) public ticker tapes cannot distinguish algorithmic cohorts with similar
learning rates (Theorem 2.3); (ii) finite observation windows force mandatory surveillance
abstention near critical stability thresholds (Theorem 2.5); and (iii) stable pricing eigenvalues
do not guarantee dealer solvency under non-normal transient magnification (Theorem 2.6).
Mitigating systemic fragility requires complementing public surveillance with audited mem-
ory buffers, pre-trade capital checks, and synchronized audit trails.
