## 1. Theorem 4.5 and Proposition 4.6

**The lower-bound mechanism works under explicit conditional assumptions.** In S28, the Gauss and Gauss–Radau measures match moments from order zero through $2K-2$. Taylor expansion at a common history cancels those terms and bounds the aggregate flow gap by $2WB(h)\Delta^{2K-1}/(2K-1)!$. The adaptive likelihood calculation also works if both models start from the same observed history or common initial law, the probe is predictable given the record and fresh independent randomization, and next-period news is conditionally Gaussian with the stated common variance. With those hypotheses and a common bound on the derivative envelope's second moment, the cost constant displayed in the theorem follows from the Gaussian KL formula and Pinsker's inequality. If the record starts under economy-specific stationary laws, the proof needs to address initial information explicitly. A fixed common-history record is the clean statement supported by S28.

**This is a worst-case lower bound in the type-parameter radius $\Delta$.** The abstract and introduction use language implying a matching order for every smooth family and every observation regime. A family with $f_\theta(h)=g(h)$ for all (	heta) is smooth and gives exactly the same aggregate flow for every population of the same mass. No number of observations separates the pair. Spell out that the lower bound uses $W_1$-separated populations and requires a bound on $\mathbb E[B(h_t)^2]$ that stays controlled along the experiment sequence. A constant that grows with more aggressive probes can change the rate. Smooth bounded flow alone does not bound derivatives with respect to a memory parameter over arbitrary histories. Use the moment hypothesis directly for the saturating example unless a derivative envelope is proved.

**Proposition 4.6 is a valid conditional recovery statement for its fixed-history flow experiment.** The minimum-distance argument is sound if the minimum is attained, for example on a compact fixed-mass class with continuous $G$. Alternatively, include an optimization-tolerance term. The rate also requires a separation constant $c_0$ that does not vanish with $\Delta$. A finite set of imposed histories with noisy flow observations is a different experiment from passive prices or trading probes that merely influence histories. State which experiment attains the rate. A local numerical rank check at one center supplies evidence about that local derivative matrix and leaves the required inverse inequality unproved.

**Numerical check.** I reran the existing script in a temporary directory. Response-gap slopes reproduced at roughly 2.98–3.07 for $K=2$ and 4.97–5.07 for $K=3$. For $K=3$, the information slope for the power-law family changed from the committed NaN to 6.03 against the predicted 10. Hump-shaped and smoothed-window slopes changed from about 9.34 and 9.29 to 10.28 and 10.36. The script's `ok` flag checks response slopes and numerical rank, and does not test the information slopes. Retain the response-gap evidence and remove the information slopes from the support for Proposition 4.6 until there is a stable calculation. The current code calculates efficient information for the first weight by inverting a near-singular Gram matrix. A high-precision derivative and an orthogonal projection against nuisance directions would test the same quantity more directly. [Numerical script](https://github.com/Pratikn03/Fianince/blob/9b7043f2/research_extension/universal_law/universal_law_check.py)

## 2. Theorem 5.1

**Part (i) works with known parameters and zero initial group states.** Each group state is the stated finite convolution of the observed history. Make it explicit that the memory rates are known along with the weights. Given the maintained independence of the next innovation, the two conditional means are then identical.

**Part (ii) can be stated cleanly as a separate stationary result.** Section 5 opens with zero initial states, while the proof of part (ii) switches to an infinite stationary prehistory and invokes part (i) for the oracle. These are distinct observation regimes. Under stationary initialization,
$$
y_{i,t}=\eta_i\sum_{j=0}^{t}r_i^j x_{t-j}+r_i^{t+1}y_{i,-1}.
$$
The observed composition record contains $q_i y_{i,t}$, so the oracle still has MSE $\sigma^2$ directly from the transition equation. There is no need to assert that a finite stationary price history reconstructs the group state.

**A source-backed route for the stationary AR expansion.** I verified the 2003 publisher record and checked the expansion in Proposition 2 and section 2 of the authors' 2005 paper, where they restate it from the 2003 result. The companion paper specifies conditions on the AR coefficients, innovation distribution, moments and allowable order range. I could not inspect the complete 2003 article through the publisher. Use the companion's directly verifiable sufficient conditions or give the precise original theorem and verify its hypotheses explicitly. One clean version assumes independent identically distributed Gaussian innovations, a stationary solution for a fixed population, $S<1$, and $L_T=\lceil c\log T\rceil$ in an admissible candidate-order range. The geometric coefficients satisfy the weighted summability condition, and the AR polynomial is nonvanishing on the closed unit disk by $\sum_j|\phi_j|=S<1$. Gaussian innovations satisfy the distribution regularity and moment conditions in the companion statement.

The variance of the best order $L$ projection error above the innovation variance is no greater than the variance of the forecast error obtained by retaining the true first $L$ AR coefficients. The latter excess is bounded by the maximum spectral density, with the normalization factor, times $\sum_{j>L}\phi_j^2$. This gives the geometric tail bound. The constant also depends on the stability margin and the innovation scale. Include those dependencies in the statement of the bound. For $c\log(1/\bar r)>1/2$, the tail is negligible relative to $L_T/T$ for a fixed population. The resulting excess for the specified least-squares predictor is $\sigma^2 L_T/T\,[1+o(1)]$. This supplies a pointwise asymptotic upper bound on the value of the additional record for a forecaster who could use that predictor. It does not establish a uniform near-collision bound when memory spacing shrinks with sample size, a finite-sample inequality of $\sigma^2 L_T/T$, or a decline in forecast value as memory rates become closer. [Ing and Wei (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0047-259X(02)00029-5) · [Ing and Wei (2005), Proposition 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0602326)

**The observation mismatch with P1F is economically substantive.** Section 2 defines $x_t=p_t-p_t^*$, the pricing error, while P1F forecasts traded-price returns. Even if traders use a common pricing-error signal, the exact irrelevance proof only applies to an observer who sees that signal or can reconstruct it from the public history. For example, let the observed level be $p_t=x_t+f_t$, where $x_t$ follows a stationary AR(1) with nonzero persistence and $f_t$ is independent Gaussian observation noise. A flow tag that reveals $x_t$ can improve the forecast of $p_{t+1}$ even when it is entirely determined by the traders' common signal $x_t$. State P1F as an empirical forecast comparison related to the benchmark, or add the bridge from public traded prices to the model signal and a condition on fundamental innovations.

## 3. Proposition 5.3 and the policy claims

- **Part (a) needs an explicit timing example.** The current sentence does not establish how the fundamental innovation enters the target or what the public record already reveals through impact. A transparent existence example sets feedback to zero and takes $x_t=Z_t$ with $Z_t$ iid. At date $t$, two informed groups with offsetting hedging needs take positions $+\beta Z_{t+1}$ and $-\beta Z_{t+1}$. Their flow cancels in public aggregates. A noiseless tag reveals $Z_{t+1}$, so the gain over the public forecast is (\operatorname{Var}$Z_{t+1}$>0). This is a construction with specified flows, independent innovations and observation timing. It requires no claim about optimal trading or equilibrium.
- **Part $b$ works under the observation assumptions used in the proof.** State in the proposition that both observers know the recoverable feedback part, that aggregate flow is observed without contamination from unobserved dealer or noise orders, that the augmented observer sees component flows, and that initial conditions add no information about future exogenous flow. Under those assumptions, prices add no information about future exogenous flow given the history of the total, because the remaining price innovations are independent of the exogenous process. The loss reduction equals the difference in projection errors multiplied by $\kappa^2$. Prior work already treats disaggregates as inputs when forecasting an aggregate. [Hendry and Hubrich (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1198/jbes.2009.07112)
- **Parts (c) and (d) concern distinct outcomes.** The stress result gives exact invariance until hard limits are reached and a probabilistic bound over finite histories. It also gives identification of a signed capacity measure under the stated response design. The targeted action result compares counterfactual feedback budgets. Remove wording that lists these results as an exhaustive set of conditions in which composition has value.
- **The last tag.** Theorem 6.3 shows that the last tag does not improve the leading dependence on memory spacing for weight recovery in the specified experiment. It can improve precision or alter the cost of a decision near its threshold. The more precise phrase is “Tagging all but one memory class removes the spacing penalty for recovering weights in this model.” Keep this distinction in the abstract and conclusion.

## 5. Positioning and proposed replacement abstract

The rate analogy is real. Heinrich and Kahn study local Wasserstein estimation in finite mixtures, and Batenkov, Goldman and Yomdin recover clustered source locations and amplitudes in a different observation experiment. Neither comparison is a claim that those articles study a financial feedback market. De Long et al. explain how positive-feedback trading affects price dynamics when rational speculators can anticipate it. Your question concerns what the market record can tell the supervisor about the effects of changing the rule for all traders versus a particular class. Lux and Zwinkels' evidence of difficult estimation motivates that question without identifying the source of the difficulty. Sanders, Irwin and Merrin's result is specific to predictive tests using position reports in ten agricultural futures markets. It is consistent with the benchmark without uniquely establishing it. [Heinrich and Kahn](https://doi.org/10.1214/17-AOS1641) · [Batenkov et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.09186v2) · [De Long et al.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1990.tb03695.x) · [Lux and Zwinkels](https://research.vu.nl/ws/files/129130045/LuxZwinkels2018_Empirical_validation_of_agentbased_models.pdf) · [Sanders et al.](https://doi.org/10.22004/ag.econ.54547)

**Proposed abstract for the revised theory paper**

> An exchange can respond to reinforcing order flow by reducing feedback across the market or by restricting a particular class of traders. We study what the public record reveals about the effects of these actions. When traders follow a common public signal, the aggregate flow response determines the market outcomes in our model. A common reduction can be evaluated from aggregate feedback. A class-specific restriction can meet a stability criterion in one population and fail it in another whose public record is nearly indistinguishable. For a smooth one-parameter family and a specified Gaussian observation experiment, we construct $K$-type populations within radius $\Delta$ that require at least order $\Delta^{-(4K-2)}$ observations to distinguish, under a moment bound on the admissible histories. In the exponential-memory model, observing class-tagged flows removes the dependence of the weight-recovery rate on the distance between memory rates once all but one class is tagged. The results explain why estimating the market's response can be easier than deciding whose trading to restrict.

**Highest credible ceiling.** A revised specialized theory paper has a defensible contribution now. The handoff gives no reproducible rubric for its numeric ratings. For a general-interest finance paper, the stronger next result is a quantitative comparison of intervention decisions given the same market record with and without class tags. A convincing empirical version would use a documented change that affected a class and compare pre-event predictions of the market response. A theory-led version would put the cost of an unsafe halt or an unnecessarily broad restriction into the same decision problem and show which records justify paying for disclosure. H1 can test whether account records help separate response times. P1F can test forecast performance in its specified panel. Each can motivate the intervention result, while the intervention itself requires distinct evidence or theory.
