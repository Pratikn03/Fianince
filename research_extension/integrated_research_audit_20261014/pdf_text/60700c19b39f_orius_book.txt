Foreword
The history of safety engineering can be read as a long sequence of moments
at which a hidden assumption became visible. The Tay Bridge fell because
no one had calculated the wind. The Tacoma Narrows oscillated because
no one had modeled aerodynamic flutter. The Therac-25 killed because no
one had verified the race condition. In each case, the engineers were not
negligent; they were operating against an assumption so deeply embedded
in their practice that its existence was invisible until disaster made it
explicit.
This monograph is about one such hidden assumption in modern
Physical AI systems: the assumption that what a controller observes is,
in some operationally meaningful sense, what the world actually is. The
assumption is so ubiquitous that we have not previously noticed making it.
It underlies battery dispatch optimizers, autonomous vehicle perception
stacks, industrial process controllers, intensive-care unit monitors, robotic
navigation systems, and aerospace flight envelope protection. In every case,
a controller proposes an action, evaluates whether that action is safe by
checking the observed state against a constraint set, and dispatches the
action when the check passes. The implicit logical step is: “the observation
reflects reality, so safety against the observation is safety against reality.”
This step is wrong, in a precise and measurable way, whenever the telemetry
channel degrades.
I came to this problem through the unglamorous route of looking at
real grid data. Two years of hourly measurements from the German power
system, taken at face value by an otherwise well-designed battery dispatch
controller, showed a peculiar pattern: the controller believed it had achieved
zero safety violations across seventeen thousand hours of operation, while
simultaneous reconstruction of the true battery state (using a simulation
calibrated against the same data) revealed six hundred and seventy-eight
occasions on which the true state-of-charge had crossed a physical safety

boundary. The controller had not malfunctioned. The optimization had
not failed. The safety check had passed every time. And yet the battery
had been at risk, on average, once every twenty-six hours of nominally safe
operation.
This phenomenon, which I call theObservation–Action Safety Gap, is
the subject of this book. The gap is not a bug to be patched or a special case
to be handled. It is a structural property of any closed-loop system in which
sensors can degrade and a controller evaluates safety against what it sees. It
existsineveryphysicalAIsystem. Itismathematicallyunavoidablewithout
a quality-aware runtime mechanism. And it is, fortunately, closeable.
Thisbookpresentsboththediagnosisandthecure. PartIformalizesthe
gap and proves its universality. Part II constructs the runtime mechanism—
which I callDC3S, the Drift-Calibrated Conformal Safety Shield—that
closes it. Part III develops the formal theory: a ladder of eighteen theorems
establishing the existence of the gap, the necessity of quality awareness, the
structural separation from belief-state methods, and the precise conditions
under which the framework transfers across physical domains. Part IV
validates the framework empirically across six domains using real public
datasets. Part V discusses the broader implications for the engineering of
safety-critical Physical AI.
The book is written for several audiences. For control theorists and
formal-methods researchers, the central contribution is the theorem ladder—
particularly the No Free Safety Theorem (Chapter 7), which proves that
quality-aware runtime adaptation is mathematically necessary, not merely
useful. For machine-learning practitioners building uncertainty-quantified
systems, the contribution is the inflation law and its place in the conformal
prediction literature. For systems engineers deploying physical AI, the
contribution is the reference architecture, the certificate governance layer,
and the cross-domain validation methodology. The book attempts to serve
all three audiences without compromising on technical depth in any of
them.
If the book has a single thesis, it is the principle stated in the epigraph:
a system that cannot measure its own uncertainty cannot be trusted with
consequences. The principle is simple. The work of operationalizing it—
making the measurement precise, the response provable, and the mechanism
universal across domains—is the work of this book.

Preface
How to Read This Book
This book is organized as a single argument developed across five parts.
The argument moves from a phenomenon (the safety gap), to its formaliza-
tion (the ORIUS-MDP framework), to a constructive solution (theDC3S
pipeline), to a body of theorems (the proof ladder), to empirical validation
(the six-domain evaluation), and finally to engineering and societal impli-
cations. Each part can be read in isolation, but the cumulative argument
is what justifies the central claim that observation quality is a first-class
control variable.
For the rushed reader. Read the Foreword, Chapter 1 (Introduction),
Chapter 7 (the theorem ladder), and Chapter 13 (the six-domain results).
This gives the phenomenon, the proofs, and the evidence in approximately
fifty pages.
For the control theorist. Read Part I (Foundations) and Part III
(Theory). The interesting material is in Chapters 5–8. The empirical
chapters are confirmatory; the proofs are the contribution.
For the machine learning practitioner.Read Chapter 6 (the calibra-
tion mechanism), Chapter 10 (drift detection and conformal extensions),
and Chapter 13 (the validation results). The central technical novelty is
the reliability-adaptive conformal calibration; the rest is the system that
uses it.
For the systems engineer. Read Chapter 4 (the architecture), Chap-
ter 12 (the certificate governance layer), and Chapter 14 (the deployment

Notation and Conventions
I have tried to keep the notation consistent across all five parts. The full
notation reference is in Appendix A; the most important conventions are:
• xt denotes thetrue physical state at timet. The controller does not
have direct access toxt.
• ot denotes theobservation at time t, which the controller does have
access to.
• wt ∈ [wmin, 1] denotes theobservation qualityor reliability score—the
runtime measurement of how trustworthyot is as a proxy forxt.
• C denotes thesafety constraint set: the set of permissible true states.
• Ut(α) denotes theobservation-consistent state setat confidence level
1 − α: the set of true states compatible with the observation under
the inflated conformal model.
• at denotes the action actually issued at time t (after any safety
repair); a∗
t denotes the candidate action proposed by the upstream
controller.
• Lowercaselettersarescalarsandvectors; calligraphicletters( S, A, O, C)
are sets.
I use P[·] for probability and E[·] for expectation throughout, and I
distinguish between marginal and conditional probabilities only when the
distinction matters for the argument.

Chapter 1
The Observation–Action Safety Gap
1.1 An Anomaly in the Data
In the autumn of 2024, while validating a battery energy storage dispatch
controller against two years of real German grid telemetry, I encountered
an anomaly that I initially mistook for a software bug. The controller,
a conventional model-predictive optimizer wrapped around a six-model
forecasting ensemble, was reporting zero safety violations across the entire
seventeen-thousand-hour evaluation. Every dispatched action passed every
safety check. The audit log was clean.
I knew this could not be right. The Open Power System Data archive
for Germany—the primary input to the controller—contains, like every real

2.4 PartiallyObservableMarkovDecisionProcesses
Themostsubtlerelationshipintheliteratureisbetween Oriusandpartially
observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs). On the surface, the two
address the same problem: control under noisy observation. A POMDP
maintains a belief statebt ∈ ∆(S), updates it via Bayes’ rule with a fixed
emission model P (ot|xt), and chooses actions to optimize an expected
reward criterion under the belief. The mathematical framework is well-
developed, with classical algorithms (value iteration, point-based methods)
and modern extensions (POMCP, DESPOT) that scale to large state
spaces.
A reasonable initial reaction to the OASG is to ask: “Why not just
model this as a POMDP?” The answer is that you can—but the resulting
POMDP does not produce the correct safety behavior under the fault types
the OASG concerns. The reason is structural and important enough to
warrant a full theorem (T5, Chapter 7), but I will sketch the argument
here.
Consider a 30-step staleness fault, in which the observation channel
freezes and the controller receives the same valueot[0] for thirty consecutive
steps while the true state evolves according to the actual dynamics. A
POMDP with a Gaussian emission modelP (ot|xt) = N (xt, σ2
o) interprets
this stream of identical observations as strong evidence that the true state
is approximately equal toot[0]. The likelihood is maximized atxt ≈ ot[0];
the prediction step widens the belief by the process noise; the update
step narrows it toward the observation. The net effect, after thirty steps
of identical observations, is a posterior that isnarrower than the prior,
concentrated near ot[0]. The POMDP is becomingmore confident as the
situation becomesmore dangerous.
The Orius response to the same fault is structurally opposite. The
OQE module detects the staleness through the freshness counter,wt drops,
the inflation factor rises, and the uncertainty setUt(α) widens. The system
becomes less confident as the situation becomes more dangerous, which is
the correct response.

3.2 The Observation Channel
The central object that distinguishesOrius from standard control-theoretic

Definition 3.3 (Observation Function). An observation function is a
measurable map g : S × W × F → O where:
• O ⊆ Rn is theobservation space, having the same dimensionality as

perfectly (wt = 1, no active fault), the observation equals the true state.
This is the condition that controllers implicitly assume always holds; the
OASG is the consequence of assuming it without checking.

Definition 3.4(Observation Quality). The observation quality or reliabil-

3.3 Fault Taxonomy
The framework distinguishes six canonical fault types, chosen because they
jointly cover the failure modes observed in real cyber-physical telemetry.
Definition 3.5(Telemetry Fault Taxonomy). A telemetry fault at time t

from a stationary state without additional metadata.
F (3) Delay: the observation is generated at timet but arrives at the
controller at timet + τ, τ > 0. The controller acts on stale data.
F (4) Spike: an outlier observationot = xt + ηt with |ηt| ≫ σ, where σ is
the nominal noise scale.

6.3.2 Why linear?
The default inflation law is linear in(1 − wt). There are other reasonable
choices—a square-root form (which corresponds to a Hoeffding-style con-
centration), an exponential form (which is more aggressive), a logistic form
(which has a soft saturation). I have experimented with all four and the
linear form is the operational best compromise: it is interpretable (the
coefficients are dimensionless and tunable), it satisfies all four properties
of the previous section, and it produces the lowest cost in the empirical
evaluation.
The square-root (Hoeffding) form is analyzed in Chapter 7 as an
alternative that produces tighter theoretical bounds in the finite-sample
regime; the empirical results (Chapter 13) show the linear and square-root
forms produce indistinguishable safety outcomes, with the linear form
having a slightly lower intervention rate.
6.4 The Connection to Inflation in Conformal Pre-
diction
The reader familiar with the conformal prediction literature will recognize
the inflation law as a runtime form ofcoverage adjustment. Adaptive
Conformal Inference (ACI) and Fully Adaptive Conformal Inference (FACI)
adjust the levelα over time based on miscoverage feedback; the inflation
law adjusts the conformal interval over time based on observation quality
feedback. The two are complementary rather than competing. In the
reference implementation, both are active simultaneously: FACI handles
slow distribution shift in the underlying signal, while the inflation law
handles fast quality degradation in the observation channel.
6.4.1 Why both are needed
A reasonable question is why two adaptation mechanisms are needed, rather
than one. The answer is that they respond to different time scales and
different signals.
FACI updates α based on a feedback signal (whether the previous
interval covered) that has a delay of one step and a noisy update direction.

9.3 The Limits of the Theory
A book about formal guarantees has a duty to be honest about what those
guarantees do not cover. This section enumerates the explicit limits of the

=== PHYSICAL PAGE 110 ===

theory, so that no reader walks away with a stronger impression of the
framework’s reach than the theorems actually justify.
9.3.1 Marginal versus conditional coverage
The coverage guarantees in the theorems are marginal: they hold on av-
erage over the input distribution, not pointwise. Conditional coverage
is provably impossible in general for finite calibration sets without para-
metric assumptions. The Mondrian extension (Chapter 6, Section 6.2.1)
provides group-conditional coverage as the strongest achievable surrogate,
but pointwise conditional coverage is not claimed.
The practical implication: there may be specific operating regimes
(extreme weather, unusual demand patterns, rare fault combinations) for
which the empirical coverage of the conformal interval is below the nominal
level, even though the average coverage across the entire deployment
satisfies the bound. The Mondrian analysis in Chapter 13 reports per-bin
coverage to make these regime-specific gaps visible.
9.3.2 Single-step versus multi-step safety
The Core Safety Bound (T3) is a one-step result: it bounds the probability
that the next true state is inC. It does not bound the probability that the
next k states are all inC for k > 1. Multi-step safety (which is what most
operational settings actually care about) is a stronger property that requires
either (a) chaining single-step bounds with additional assumptions about
the controller dynamics, or (b) developing a multi-step receding-horizon
variant ofDC3S.
The practical implication: the single-step bound is sufficient when the
upstream controller has good closed-loop properties (so that one safe action
leads to a state from which the next safe action is also reachable). It is
insufficient for systems where the controller may be lured into a corner of
the state space from which no future action is safe. Closed-loop stability
of the controller-plus-shield combination is a separate question that the
framework does not address.

9.3.3 Adversarial completeness
The Byzantine-resistant OQE (Chapter 5, Section 5.5) handles adversaries
that compromise fewer than 1/3 of the readings in the OQE window.
An adversary who can compromise more than this fraction, or who can
inject biases that are statistically indistinguishable from clean telemetry,
is outside the threat model. The framework does not claim to be secure
against arbitrary attackers; it claims to be secure against the specific threat
model defined by the Byzantine bound.
9.3.4 Hardware-in-the-loop validation
The empirical results in Part IV are obtained from software-in-the-loop
replay against real public datasets. The datasets are real, the controller
code is real, and the fault injection is deterministic and reproducible—but
the closed-loop is replay-based, not connected to physical actuators. A
complete hardware-in-the-loop validation program (battery testbed, AV
testbed, aerospace HIL rig) is identified in Chapter 15 as the critical path for
deployment-gradecertification. TheOASGphenomenonisapropertyofthe
telemetry-controller interface, which SIL captures faithfully; nevertheless,
hardware deployment introduces additional engineering concerns (latency,
actuator dynamics, timing) that the framework does not address.
9.3.5 Aging, calibration drift, and long-term operation
The framework assumes the calibration set is exchangeable with the de-
ployment data. Over long deployments, this assumption can fail through
model aging, sensor drift, and distributional shift in the underlying signals.
The FACI mechanism (Chapter 6) provides one form of online recalibra-
tion, but it is not a complete solution for long-term deployment. Periodic
recalibration against fresh held-out data is recommended in operational de-
ployments, with retraining triggers driven by the monitoring infrastructure
of Chapter 14.

10.4 The Unified Fault Schedule
All six domains are evaluated under a shared fault schedule parameterized
by three probabilities:
• Dropout probability: 0.15 per step (the observation channel re-
turns ∅).
• Spike probability: 0.08 per step (a single-channel outlier with
magnitude drawn fromN (0, 4σ2)).
• Staleness probability: 0.10 per step (the observation channel
freezes for a burst of duration drawn from Poisson(5)).
Per-step faults are drawn independently, with the constraint that dropout
and staleness are mutually exclusive (a channel cannot simultaneously drop
and freeze). Delay, drift, and reorder faults occur at lower rates as domain-
specific injections, calibrated from the observed failure characteristics of
the telemetry in each domain.
The fault schedule is generated once per evaluation from a fixed seed
(seed = 42) and applied identically to every controller under comparison.
This gives paired comparisons with reduced variance: each controller is
evaluated on the same sequence of faults.

11.6 The TTC Predictive Entry Barrier
Before leaving the battery domain, I want to sketch one methodological
insight that will recur in Chapter 12 for the autonomous vehicle adapter:
the importance of evaluating the safety predicate on thepost-action state
rather than the current state.
In the battery domain, this is straightforward. The SOC dynamics are
first-order: SOCt+1 = SOCt + η · Pt · ∆t, and the constraintSOCt+1 ∈
[20, 180] is evaluated directly on the next-step SOC. The shield projects
the power Pt onto the set of values for whichSOCt+1 lies in the constraint.
This is what Condition (R) of the Transfer Theorem (T11) requires: the
repair action must produce a safe next state.
In the autonomous vehicle domain (Chapter 12), this same principle
requires an adaptation. A naive constraint formulation would check the

The fix is the predictive Time-to-Collision (TTC) barrier:

The universality result has three implications worth stating explicitly.
The OASG is a property of the telemetry-controller interface, not
the physical domain. If the gap were a battery-specific phenomenon,
it would not replicate across six radically different systems with different
state spaces, different dynamics, different sensors, different constraints,
and different failure modes. The fact that it does replicate is evidence that
the gap is a property of the interface itself: whenever a controller checks
safety against an observation rather than against ground truth, the gap
can appear.
The Transfer Theorem is a powerful organizing principle.All
five non-battery adapters satisfy the three conditions of T11, and in each
case the safety guarantees follow by direct application of the theorem. No
per-domain proof is required. This is the operational power of the theorem:
it reduces the validation of each new domain from a research problem to
an engineering check.
The intervention rate is the right metric to watch.The TSVR
tells us whether the shield is working; the intervention rate tells us how
expensive the shield is. Across the six domains, IR ranges from 2.8%
(battery) to 100% (navigation). The variation reflects the tightness of the
operational margins in each domain, not any difference in the framework
itself. Domains with tight margins (industrial, navigation, aerospace) have
high IR because even small OQE-driven margin increases force the shield
to adjust almost every action. Domains with looser margins (battery, AV,
healthcare) have lower IR because many candidate actions already satisfy
the tightened constraints. Both cases are valid: the shield does its job in
both, and the framework provides the operational guarantee in both.

14.3.1 Invariant I1: No dispatch without a valid certificate
Every action issued by the system must be accompanied by a valid certifi-
cate. A valid certificate is one for which:
• the hash ht is correctly computed from the certificate fields;
• the previous-hash linkht−1 matches the previous certificate’s hash;
• the validity horizonHt is non-zero;
• the status is notREVOKED.
If any of these conditions fails, the certificate is invalid and the action
cannot be dispatched. The enforcement is implemented as a gate in the
dispatch path: the pipeline’s output is routed through the certificate
validator before it reaches the physical actuator interface.
14.3.2 Invariant I2: Hash chain integrity
Every certificate’sht−1 field points to the previous certificate in the chain.
Tampering with any certificate breaks the chain downstream:ht depends
on ht−1, so changing an earlier certificate invalidates every subsequent one.
The audit ledger writer verifies the chain on every append and raises an
alert if a break is detected.
The chain integrity property is what makes the certificate log useful as
a forensic artifact. If a safety incident occurs, the investigator can walk
the chain from the incident back to the beginning of the deployment and
verify that every certificate is legitimate. If an attacker attempts to modify
the chain, the modification is detectable.
14.3.3 Invariant I3: Automatic fallback on horizon expira-
tion
When the validity horizonHt reaches zero and recovery has not occurred,
the certificate expires. The governance layer automatically invokes the
fallback policy πf b and marks subsequent certificates with statusFALL-
BACK. The system continues operating on the fallback until telemetry
recovers and a valid non-fallback certificate can be issued.
This automatic fallback is the operational expression of Assumption
A8 (admissible fallback exists). The assumption says such a fallback exists;
the governance layer ensures it is actually invoked when needed.

14.4 The Reproducibility Infrastructure
The reproducibility infrastructure is the operational expression of the

14.4.1 The evaluation manifest
The manifest is a YAML document inreproducibility/manifests/ that

reproduce it, the expected runtime, and the SHA-256 hash of the expected
output. A reader who runs the commands and obtains a different hash

or random seed) and can investigate.

14.4.2 The locked evaluation runner
The locked runner is a driver script that takes a manifest entry ID, executes
the specified command in a clean environment, computes the hash of the
output, and compares it to the expected hash. If the hashes match, the
result is confirmed. If they differ, the runner emits a diff report showing
which fields changed and by how much, so the investigator can identify the
source of the discrepancy.
The runner uses Docker for environment isolation. The exact Docker
image hash is part of the manifest entry, which ensures that the dependency
versions (NumPy, PyTorch, LightGBM, and so on) are identical to those
used during the book’s evaluation.

14.4.3 The hash chain over results
Every locked evaluation produces a results tuple (metric name, value,
confidence interval, hash). The tuples are themselves chained: each result’s
hash includes the hash of the previous result, producing a linked sequence
that verifies the integrity of the full evaluation run. This is structurally
identical to the certificate chain inCertOS, applied to the evaluation
outputs rather than runtime dispatches.

The ORIUS-Bench seven metrics (TSVR, OASG, CVA, GDQ, IR, AC, RL)
are exposed to the operational monitoring stack via Prometheus-compatible
gauges and histograms. The monitoring stack is straightforward:
• Prometheus scrapes the kernel’s metric endpoint every 15 seconds
and stores the scalar metrics (gauges forwt, inflt, Ht; counters for

• Alertmanager fires alerts on threshold crossings: a TSVR above

The monitoring stack is the operational layer through which human
operators interact with a running deployment. It is not part of the formal
framework (the theorems do not depend on the monitoring stack), but it
is essential for real deployments because it is how operators learn that the
framework is working or that intervention is needed.

Chapter 15
Limitations, Future Work, and
Implications
Every research contribution has boundaries, and honesty about those
boundaries is what distinguishes a durable contribution from an overreach.
This chapter enumerates the limitations ofOrius, the future work I see
as most important, and the broader implications of treating observation
quality as a first-class control variable.
15.1 Limitations
15.1.1 The framework is a one-step safety filter
The Core Safety Bound (T3) guarantees that the next-step true state is inC
with high probability. It does not directly guarantee multi-step trajectories.
If the upstream controller has pathological closed-loop behavior—for ex-
ample, oscillating near a constraint boundary—the shield can successfully
protect each individual step while the trajectory as a whole remains in a
dangerous regime.
The practical implication: DC3S is sufficient when the upstream
controller has well-behaved closed-loop dynamics (stable MPC, Lyapunov-
stable CBF, converged RL policy). It is insufficient as a substitute for
closed-loop stability analysis. A multi-step extension via receding-horizon
DC3S is the most important future work direction and is sketched in
Section 15.2.
15.1.2 Marginal versus conditional coverage
The coverage guarantees are marginal: they hold on average over the input
distribution, not pointwise. Conditional coverage is provably impossible
133

=== PHYSICAL PAGE 162 ===
Chapter 15. Limitations, Future Work, and Implications 134
for distribution-free conformal prediction without parametric assumptions
(Lei et al. 2018). The Mondrian extension provides group-conditional
coverage across reliability bins and volatility regimes, which is the strongest
achievable surrogate, but strict pointwise coverage is not claimed.
The practical implication: specific unusual operating regimes (extreme
weather, rare fault combinations, outlier demand patterns) may exhibit
lower empirical coverage than the nominal level. The monitoring stack
should track per-regime coverage and alert on regime-specific gaps.
15.1.3 Adversarial completeness
The Byzantine-resistant OQE handles adversaries that compromise fewer
than 1/3 of the readings in the OQE window. An adversary who can
compromise more than this fraction, or who can inject biases that are
statistically indistinguishable from clean telemetry, is outside the threat
model. The framework is designed for fault-tolerance, not for security
against a sophisticated, coordinated attacker.
The practical implication: deployments in cyber-physical security con-
texts (critical infrastructure, defense) should pairOrius with additional
security-focused defenses (sensor authentication, encrypted telemetry, intru-
sion detection). The framework is one layer of a defense-in-depth strategy,
not the entire strategy.
15.1.4 Aging, drift, and recalibration
The calibration set is assumed to be exchangeable with the deployment
data. Over long deployments, this assumption can fail through model
aging, sensor drift, and distributional shift in the underlying signals. The
FACI mechanism provides one form of online recalibration, but it is not a
complete solution for long-term deployment without human oversight.
The practical implication: operational deployments should schedule
periodic recalibration against fresh held-out data, with retraining triggers
driven by the monitoring stack. A three-to-six-month recalibration cadence
is reasonable for most domains; faster cadences may be needed in domains
with rapid distribution shift.

15.1.5 Hardware-in-the-loop validation
The empirical results in Part IV are from software-in-the-loop replay
against real public datasets. The datasets are real and the fault injection
is deterministic, but the closed-loop is replay-based, not connected to
physical actuators. A complete hardware-in-the-loop validation program is
the critical path for deployment-grade certification.
The practical implication: results in this book should be read as the
SIL baseline against which HIL validation can be measured. The OASG
phenomenon is a property of the telemetry-controller interface, which
SIL captures faithfully; but hardware deployment introduces additional
engineering concerns (actuator dynamics, timing jitter, network latency)
that require separate characterization.
15.1.6 Multi-agent composition
The framework is validated on single-agent systems. Multi-agent composi-
tions (fleets of batteries sharing a grid constraint, squadrons of autonomous
vehicles in coordinated maneuvers, networks of medical devices on shared
patients) introduce coupling effects that single-agent safety guarantees do
not cover. A Non-Composition result (that individual safe controllers do
not compose into a safe multi-agent system without explicit coordination)
is known in the coordination literature; extendingOrius to multi-agent
settings requires the coordination primitives that are beyond the scope of
this book.

15.3 Broader Implications
The Orius framework, if taken seriously, has implications beyond its
technical content. I want to close the book with three that I think deserve
attention.
15.3.1 Observation quality as a first-class control variable
The framework treatswt as a measurable runtime quantity on par with
the true statext and the actionat. This is a conceptual shift. In classical
control theory, observation is implicit: the controller is assumed to have
accesstothestate, ortohaveaccesstoanobservationwithknownstatistical
properties. The observation channel is not itself part of the state.Orius
makes the channel state explicit.
The implication for system design is thatevery physical AI system
should instrument its telemetry channel with runtime quality monitoring,
not just those systems with known fault modes. The monitoring is cheap
(sub-millisecond per step); the payoff is that the safety reasoning becomes
honest about what the system actually knows rather than what it assumes.
15.3.2 The difference between calibration-time and runtime
assumptions
A recurring theme in this book is the distinction between assumptions
that hold at calibration time (when the models were trained and the safety
margins were set) and assumptions that hold at runtime (when the system
is actually operating). The classical approach assumes that calibration-
time assumptions extend to runtime; the OASG is the consequence of that
assumption failing.
The implication for machine learning practice is that any system de-
ployed in the real world needs a mechanism to detect when runtime
conditions have diverged from calibration-time conditions, and to respond
appropriately. This is true not only for safety-critical systems but for any
ML system where the cost of being wrong is higher than the cost of being
cautious. Conformal prediction is one such mechanism;Orius is another,
specifically tuned for safety rather than pure statistical coverage.

=== PHYSICAL PAGE 167 ===
139 15.3. Broader Implications
15.3.3 Trust in deployed AI systems
The deepest implication is about the conditions under which deployed AI
systems can be trusted. A system that cannot measure its own uncertainty
cannot be trusted with consequences, because its confidence is uncorrelated
with its correctness. A system that can measure its own uncertainty—and
whose downstream actions respond appropriately to that measurement—
can be trusted in a stronger sense: its confidence is a calibrated signal
about its reliability.
This is the operational content of the ORIUS Principle. It is not a
slogan; it is a design discipline. Systems that follow the discipline are
trustworthy in the sense that their behavior is predictable from their
self-reported state. Systems that do not follow the discipline are not
trustworthy, regardless of how sophisticated their internal models are,
because there is no way to tell from the outside whether they are working
correctly or failing silently.
The question for the coming decade of Physical AI deployment is not
whether quality-aware runtime safety is needed. The evidence is in: it is
needed, and the No Free Safety Theorem proves that the need is structural
rather than incidental. The question is how quickly the discipline can be
adopted in the systems that currently operate without it.

16.1 What Was Done
This book began with an anomaly. A battery dispatch controller, running
on two years of real German grid data, reported zero safety violations
while the underlying physical state had crossed its safety boundaries on 678
separate occasions. The controller was observationally safe and physically
unsafe. The gap between these two facts—the gap between what the
controller saw and what was actually true—was the subject of everything
that followed.
In five parts, the book did four things:
Identified the phenomenon. The Observation–Action Safety Gap is
a structural property of any closed-loop physical AI system in which a
controller evaluates safety against observed telemetry that can degrade. It
is not a bug in any particular system; it is a consequence of how safety
is usually computed. The empirical demonstration in Chapter 1 (3.90%
hidden violations on the battery domain) and the universal demonstration
in Chapter 12 (baseline rates from 3.90% to 21.53% across six domains)
establish that the phenomenon is real and widespread.
Formalized the framework. Chapter 3 developed the ORIUS-MDP as
an extension of the standard MDP framework with runtime observation
quality as a first-class variable. The formal apparatus of Part III—eighteen
theorems, seven propositions, ten corollaries, twenty-five definitions, eight
standing assumptions—provides the complete mathematical foundation on
which the runtime mechanism rests. The Core Safety Bound (T3) quantifies
the relationship between observation reliability and achievable physical
safety; the No Free Safety Theorem (T4) proves that quality awareness is a

the framework. Each stage has a specific role, each depends on the outputs
