Abstract
Can public market data identify destabilizing algorithmic feedback, and when does
feedback threaten dealer solvency? We establish two fundamental limits. First, public
price–flow data identify only merged rate–weight representations, with anΩ(δ−4)sam-

tion windows force mandatory surveillance abstention near critical stability thresholds.
Empirically, measured feedback is dominated by filtering choices: raw-return feedback
intervals span zero, and covariance restrictions reject (J= 73.0,df= 4). Second, spec-
tral price stability (ρ(J)<1) does not prevent dealer balance-sheet exhaustion under
non-normal transient amplification (r∗=B/sup t≥0∥CJt∥2). Funded dealer simula-
tions illustrate that capital solvency fails inside the spectrally stable regime, motivating
capital-aware pre-trade gating.

1 Introduction
The delegation of financial execution and market making to autonomous algorithms has fun-
damentally transformed the microstructural physics of modern electronic exchanges. Over
the past three decades, financial markets have undergone an unprecedented structural tran-
sition from human-intermediated floor trading and manual specialist posts (such as the
historic open-outcry pits of Chicago and the specialist trading posts of the New York Stock
Exchange) to fully automated, electronic continuous double auctions housed in co-located
server racks in Secaucus, New Jersey (Equinix NY4) and Slough, United Kingdom (Equinix
LD4). In contemporary equity, sovereign debt, foreign exchange, and digital asset venues,
automated execution engines, statistical arbitrageurs, and high-frequency market makers
(HFMs) submit and cancel the overwhelming majority of limit quotes, respond to order
flow in microseconds, and continuously adapt their execution strategies to prevailing mar-
ket conditions (Hasbrouck, 1991; Menkveld, 2013; Budish, Cramton, and Shim, 2015; Biais,
Foucault, and Moinas, 2015).
When trading algorithms condition their order submission and quote-placement decisions
on recent transaction prices, prevailing order book imbalances, and trade innovations, the
price-formation process ceases to be an open-loop mechanism driven purely by exogenous
informational updates regarding fundamentals. Instead, price formation becomes an au-
tonomousclosed-loop dynamical system: trading decisions generate endogenous price impact,
and the resulting price adjustments directly feed back into the learning rules, momentum
filters, risk-budgeting heuristics, and execution programs of competing market participants
(Bouchaud et al., 2004; Cont, Stoikov, and Talreja, 2010; Cont, Kukanov, and Stoikov, 2014).
This closed-loop structural reality was dramatically underscored by major episodic dis-
ruptions in electronic markets over the past decade and a half. During the May 6, 2010 Flash
Crash, the E-mini S&P 500 index collapsed by more than 9% in a matter of minutes before
rebounding, triggered when an automated selling program interacted with high-frequency
market makers whose internal risk management algorithms rapidly depleted resting order
book depth. Similar self-amplifying cascades occurred during the October 15, 2014 Treasury
flash rally, the August 24, 2015 equity exchange-traded fund (ETF) pricing breakdowns, and
the catastrophic liquidity unpegs across digital asset exchanges in March 2020 and November
2022. In each of these events, markets populated by sophisticated algorithms designed to
optimize execution individually exhibited severe collective fragility.
These structural phenomena confront financial economists, central banks, and market
regulators with an urgent, foundational question:
Can observable market data identify destabilizing adaptive feedback, when does

1.1 Related Literature
Our work bridges four major strands of literature across financial economics, market mi-
crostructure, systemic risk, and dynamical systems theory.
High-Frequency Trading and Market Microstructure.Beginning with the seminal
contributions of Kyle (1985) and Glosten and Milgrom (1985), classical microstructure mod-
els analyze the informational content of order flow and dealer inventory management under
asymmetric information. Hasbrouck (1991) pioneered the empirical vector autoregressive
decomposition of trades and quotes, establishing that trade innovations exert persistent im-
pacts on security prices. Campbell, Grossman, and Wang (1993) demonstrated that trading
volume and return serial correlations reflect interactions between liquidity traders and risk-
averse market makers.
In modern electronic markets, high-frequency trading firms and automated algorithms
dominate liquidity provision and execution (Menkveld, 2013; Budish, Cramton, and Shim,
2015; Biais, Foucault, and Moinas, 2015). While Hendershott, Jones, and Menkveld (2011)
document that algorithmic trading improves liquidity and narrows bid-ask spreads during

normal trading periods, Budish, Cramton, and Shim (2015) show that continuous double
auctions generate socially wasteful latency arms races, and Biais, Foucault, and Moinas
(2015) demonstrate that algorithmic speed advantages can lead to adverse selection spirals.
We advance this literature by formalizing the closed loop between algorithmic learning rules
and endogenous price impact, establishing fundamental realization-theoretic bounds on what
econometricians can infer from public ticker feeds.
Price Impact and Limit Order Book Dynamics.A extensive literature examines the
physics of price impact in electronic limit order books (Almgren and Chriss, 2000; Bouchaud
et al., 2004; Cont, Stoikov, and Talreja, 2010; Cont, Kukanov, and Stoikov, 2014). Empirical
studies consistently reveal that signed order flow exhibits strong positive autocorrelation over
hundreds of trades (long memory), while price returns remain approximately unpredictable
(Bouchaud et al., 2004; Cont, Kukanov, and Stoikov, 2014). This apparent paradox is
resolved by subtle negative feedback in quote replacement and cancellation. Cont, Kukanov,
and Stoikov (2014) introduce Order Flow Imbalance (OFI) as a powerful predictor of short-
term price movements across multiple depth levels. We explicitly incorporate and control for
order-splitting memory in our econometric models, demonstrating that even after purging 30
to 90 lags of signed flow memory, a positive and statistically significant feedback component
persists in continuous high-frequency trading.
Intermediary Solvency, Funding Liquidity, and Systemic Risk.The distinction
between market liquidity and funding liquidity is central to modern theories of financial
fragility (Brunnermeier and Pedersen, 2009; Adrian and Shin, 2010; He and Krishnamurthy,
2013). When leveraged market makers suffer balance-sheet drawdowns, risk-management
constraints (such as Value-at-Risk limits or regulatory margin requirements) force them to
withdraw quotes, widening spreads and amplifying price dislocations (Greenwood, Landier,
and Thesmar, 2015; Eisenberg and Noe, 2001). Our analysis demonstrates that non-normal
transient amplification can trigger intermediary insolvency even when the underlying price-
formation process is spectrally stable (ρ(J)<1).

Trefethen and Embree, 2005). While eigenvalues govern asymptotic behavior ast→∞,
the pseudospectrum and the Kreiss constant determine short-term amplification. Although
non-normal operator theory has been applied in macroeconomics to examine input-output

4.3 Flagship Natural Experiment: The July 2022 Binance Fee
Overhaul
A fundamental objection in empirical microstructure is that observational estimates of feed-
back might reflect endogenous shifts in macroeconomic volatility rather than structural liq-

To address this objection decisively, we exploit an exogenous institutional shock: the
July 8, 2022 Binance Zero-Fee Trading Schedule Overhaul.
Institutional Background and Policy Shock.On July 6, 2022, Binance announced
that effective July 8, 2022 at 14:00 UTC (commemorating its fifth anniversary), it would
eliminate all maker and taker transaction fees across thirteen Bitcoin spot trading pairs,
most notably BTC/USDT. Prior to this date, standard trading fees ranged from 2 to 10
basis points depending on user VIP tier. Simultaneously, Binance adjusted how BTC trad-
ing volume counted toward VIP tier qualification and Spot Liquidity Provider programs,
effectively decoupling nominal execution fees from exchange volume subsidies.
Crucially for econometric identification:
•Treated Group:BTC/USDT transitioned to a strict 0.0% maker and 0.0% taker fee
schedule.
•Control Group:Major liquid altcoin pairs (ETH, BNB, SOL, ADA, DOGE, XRP,
LTC) traded on the identical matching engine under identical matching rules, identical
co-location latency, and identical API protocols, butretained standard maker-
taker fee schedules.
Eliminating taker fees drastically reduced execution costs for aggressive order flow. In
standardmicrostructuretheory, ifpositivefeedbackisdrivenbyshort-horizontrend-following
flow, fee elimination lowers the hurdle rate for aggressive market orders, potentially ampli-
fying closed-loop feedback.

7 Regulatory Policy Implications and Conclusion
7.1 Pre-Trade Surveillance vs. Post-Trade Circuit Breakers
Our theoretical and empirical findings carry direct implications for modern financial market
surveillance, notably SEC Rule 15c3-5 (Market Access Rule) in the United States and MiFID
II Regulatory Technical Standard 6 (RTS 6) in the European Union. Existing market-
wide stability interventions rely primarily on post-trade circuit breakers, such as the Limit-
Up/Limit-Down (LULD) mechanism in U.S. equities. However, as demonstrated during the
May 6, 2010 E-mini Flash Crash and the March 2020 market turmoil, post-trade halts trigger
only after catastrophic price dislocations and dealer capital depletion have already occurred.
Within the model, the safe-radius result motivates a capital-aware pre-trade gating rule:
exchanges dynamically pace maximum allowable order submission rates based on prevailing
depth and dealer capital, bounding aggressive flow before execution to mitigate dealer buffer
exhaustion while preserving continuous price discovery during calm trading.

7.3 MarketDesign: Continuous Auctionsvs. Frequent Batch Auc-

Our findings also speak directly to the structural debate on market design initiated by Bud-
ish, Cramton, and Shim (2015), who propose replacing continuous double auctions with
Frequent Batch Auctions (FBAs) to eliminate latency arbitrage. While FBAs eliminate
microsecond sniper races within batch intervals, they do not resolve closed-loop adaptive
feedback across batches: algorithmic momentum strategies updating across consecutive 100-
millisecond auction clearings still generate price impact loops. Kreiss-bounded rate gat-
ing complements batching by providing a continuous, state-dependent model-based capital
bound that operates effectively under both continuous matching and discrete batch auctions.
7.4 Information Missing from Public Market Feeds
Our realization-theoretic theorems prove that public market data suffer from fundamen-
tal blind spots: (i) public ticker tapes cannot distinguish algorithmic cohorts with similar
learning rates (Theorem 2.3); (ii) finite observation windows force mandatory surveillance
abstention near critical stability thresholds (Theorem 2.5); and (iii) stable pricing eigenvalues
do not guarantee dealer solvency under non-normal transient magnification (Theorem 2.6).
Mitigating systemic fragility requires complementing public surveillance with audited mem-
ory buffers, pre-trade capital checks, and synchronized audit trails.
7.5 Conclusion
We examine whether observable market data can identify destabilizing adaptive feedback
between algorithms and financial prices, and whether dynamic eigenvalue stability guar-
antees financial safety. Our findings establish two fundamental results. First, observable
feedback does not imply identifiable machine behavior: public market feeds identify only
aggregate merged rate–weight representations rather than individual algorithmic cohorts
(Theorem 2.3), active probing to distinguish nearby adaptation rates faces a severeΩ(δ−4)
sample-complexity barrier (Theorem 2.3), and empirical feedback estimates are fragile arti-
facts of observation clocks and moving-average filtering. Second, dynamic price stability does
not imply intermediary safety: because the closed-loop market Jacobian is generically non-
normal, stable eigenvalues (ρ(J)<1) can coexist with severe transient balance-sheet deple-
