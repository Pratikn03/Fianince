Abstract
We formalize the financial system identification of closed-loop algorithmic markets,
asking what public price and flow data can reveal regarding traders’ adaptive mem-
ory and at what financial cost. We prove that public ticker feeds cannot distinguish
algorithmic cohorts sharing an adaptation rate, and establishing memory separationδ
requires active executed quantity of orderδ−2or capital of orderδ−4. Passive waiting
cannot substitute for active probing when new excitation is absent. In an empirical
768-unit panel, feedback estimates are fragile to flow timing and filtering. Under finite
exchange depth and finite observation windows, active memory resolution requires cap-
ital scaling asΘ(δ−4), establishing that identification speed is bounded by exchange
liquidity.
Keywords:Adaptive learning, algorithmic trading, financial stability, market microstruc-
ture, system identification, non-normal dynamics, Kreiss constant.

1 Introduction
Electronic execution and market making raise a financial identification question: what can
aggregate price and flow observations reveal about trading rules, and which financial con-
straints govern experiments intended to learn those rules? Microstructure research docu-
ments the interaction of trades, quotes and automated liquidity provision (Hasbrouck, 1991;
Menkveld, 2013; Budish, Cramton, and Shim, 2015). We study a declared linear feedback
economy and its information/resource contracts, then test whether observable measurement
diagnostics support the corresponding interpretation.
When trading algorithms condition their order submission and quote-placement decisions
on recent transaction prices, prevailing order book imbalances, and trade innovations, the
price-formation process ceases to be an open-loop mechanism driven purely by exogenous
informational updates regarding fundamentals. Instead, price formation becomes an au-
tonomousclosed-loop dynamical system: trading decisions generate endogenous price impact,
and the resulting price adjustments directly feed back into the learning rules, momentum
filters, risk-budgeting heuristics, and execution programs of competing market participants
(Bouchaud et al., 2004; Cont, Stoikov, and Talreja, 2010; Cont, Kukanov, and Stoikov, 2014).
This closed-loop structural reality was dramatically underscored by major episodic dis-
ruptions in electronic markets over the past decade and a half. While disruptions such as the
May 6, 2010 Flash Crash in the E-mini S&P 500 and the October 15, 2014 Treasury flash
rally provide compelling historical motivation for analyzing how algorithmic feedback can
rapidly deplete liquidity, direct empirical investigation of these episodes is constrained by
the non-public nature of regulatory account registries. Modern electronic cryptocurrency or-
der books provide an ideal, transparent empirical testing ground. Electronic cryptocurrency
venues provide public trade and book data, but these records do not identify the fraction of
activity generated by adaptive algorithms. The supplied samples have the clocks and cover-
age described below; they do not establish universal nanosecond observation or the absence
of off-exchange execution. We therefore exploit high-frequency cryptocurrency order books
(complemented by a supplied equity representation whose normalized-source transformation
now reproduces exactly, while company-label mapping and physical scale remain unresolved)
as an empirical testing ground for our broader theoretical framework.
These information and financial questions concern financial economists, central banks,

Our work bridges four major strands of literature across financial economics, market mi-
crostructure, systemic risk, and dynamical systems theory.
High-Frequency Trading and Market Microstructure.Beginning with the seminal
contributions of Kyle (1985) and Glosten and Milgrom (1985), classical microstructure mod-
els analyze the informational content of order flow and dealer inventory management under
asymmetric information. Hasbrouck (1991) pioneered the empirical vector autoregressive

decomposition of trades and quotes, establishing that trade innovations exert persistent im-
pacts on security prices. Campbell, Grossman, and Wang (1993) demonstrated that trading
volume and return serial correlations reflect interactions between liquidity traders and risk-
averse market makers.
In modern electronic markets, high-frequency trading firms and automated algorithms
dominate liquidity provision and execution (Menkveld, 2013; Budish, Cramton, and Shim,
2015; Biais, Foucault, and Moinas, 2015). While Hendershott, Jones, and Menkveld (2011)
document that algorithmic trading improves liquidity and narrows bid-ask spreads during
normal trading periods, Budish, Cramton, and Shim (2015) show that continuous double
auctions generate socially wasteful latency arms races, and Biais, Foucault, and Moinas
(2015) demonstrate that algorithmic speed advantages can lead to adverse selection spirals.
We advance this literature by formalizing the closed loop between algorithmic learning rules
and endogenous price impact, establishing conditional realization and resource bounds for
the declared controlled experiment. Public ticker data require a separate observation and
identification argument.
Price Impact and Limit Order Book Dynamics.An extensive literature studies price
impact, signed-flow memory and quote imbalance (Bouchaud et al., 2004; Cont, Kukanov,
and Stoikov, 2014). Our long-run regression diagnostics retain these as competing explana-
tions. Lag controls and observable kernel signs do not identify parent orders, learner policies
or contemporaneous structural shocks.
Intermediary Solvency, Funding Liquidity, and Systemic Risk.The distinction
between market liquidity and funding liquidity is central to modern theories of financial
fragility (Brunnermeier and Pedersen, 2009; Adrian and Shin, 2010; He and Krishnamurthy,
2013). When leveraged market makers suffer balance-sheet drawdowns, risk-management
constraints (such as Value-at-Risk limits or regulatory margin requirements) force them to
withdraw quotes, widening spreads and amplifying price dislocations (Greenwood, Landier,
and Thesmar, 2015; Eisenberg and Noe, 2001). Our conditional loss-output analysis sepa-
rates spectral stability from a declared loss budget. The empirical studies do not demonstrate
intermediary insolvency in a calibrated stable market; the positive price-only response has
the more restrictive geometry proved below.

7.3 MarketDesign: Continuous Auctionsvs. Frequent Batch Auc-
tions
Our findings also speak directly to the structural debate on market design initiated by
Budish, Cramton, and Shim (2015), who propose replacing continuous double auctions with
Frequent Batch Auctions (FBAs) to eliminate latency arbitrage. Batch matching changes ex-
ecution timing and incentives; a feedback model can also be posed across successive batches.
This paper does not estimate its comparative economic effect. The finite-window quantity
bound can be examined under either matching design when its input/output and account-
ing assumptions hold. Comparative welfare, execution feasibility and effectiveness across
continuous and batch auctions remain unvalidated.

the stated innovation law and exclusion of richer financial records. Exact marked equity

The positive-class quantity certificate requires authenticated impact, margin and account
assumptions; it does not require individual learning-rate identities once that model class

7.5 Conclusion
We establish a resource distinction within a precisely defined memory-resolution experiment:
lifetime executed quantity of orderδ−2is necessary and sufficient for a known symmetric
pair, while average power gives horizon orderδ−4. Actual probe-trader execution cost gives
matching capital orderδ−4for funded deterministic probes under the restricted noisy-price
contract. A separate counterparty cash/margin contract gives a sharp quantity allowance,
including the inventory created by the probe. Exact equity disclosure and natural excitation
supply constructive counterexamples to a universal capital barrier. A fixed price-exposure
output is a separate contract. More waiting alone cannot overcome a fixed lifetime quantity
allowance; the result does not bound every tailored safe strategy. The empirical recon-
struction shows strong timing/filter sensitivity and maintained-model rejection, rather than
participant-level learning attribution. Existing simulations distinguish capacity events, de-
faults and effective dynamics. Real-market financial calibration and independent specialist
assessment remain necessary before publication and operational claims.
