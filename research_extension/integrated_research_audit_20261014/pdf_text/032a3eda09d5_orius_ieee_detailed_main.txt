I. Introduction
The deployment of AI-driven controllers in safety-
critical physical systems—battery energy storage, au-
tonomous vehicles, and clinical monitoring—has pro-
gressed from laboratory prototypes to field-deployed
assets. A pervasive but under-addressed hazard ac-
companies this transition: the controller takes actions
conditioned onobservationsof the true physical
state, yet observations are never perfect. Sensors stall,
telemetry packets arrive late or out-of-order, cali-
bration drifts silently, and network links drop entire
channels. When the observation pipeline degrades,
the gap between what the controllerbelievesto be
the state and what the stateactually ismay grow
unboundedly, and the controller may take an action
that is formally safe in the observed state space yet
catastrophically unsafe in the true state space.
We call this gap theObservation–Action Safety
Gap(OASG). The OASG is distinct from standard
notions of model uncertainty, adversarial perturba-

III. Related Work
The ORIUS framework sits at the intersection of
several research threads: runtime verification, confor-
mal prediction, safety shielding, and CPS telemetry
resilience. We survey each in turn, highlighting the
gap that ORIUS addresses.
A. Runtime Verification and Monitoring
Runtime-verification (RV) monitors check whether
an execution trace satisfies a temporal-logic specifica-
tion [2, 5]. Classic RV monitors arepassive: they flag
violations but do not modify the controller’s action.
The simplex architecture [1] introduces adecision
modulethat switches between a complex controller
and a verified-safe baseline, but it assumes that the
state is accurately known—the observation pipeline
is outside its trust model.
ORIUS goes beyond passive monitoring by actively
computing a constraint-tightened action set and
repairing unsafe actions. Crucially, it operates on
degradedobservations and inflates the safety envelope
accordingly, a capability absent from standard simplex
or watchdog designs.
B. Conformal Prediction for Safety
Conformal prediction (CP) constructs distribution-
free prediction intervals with finite-sample coverage
guarantees [6]. Adaptive Conformal Inference [ 7]
and related methods handle distribution shift online.
Recent work applies CP to robotics and motion
planning [8, 9].
ORIUS uses CP as thecalibration engine(Stage 2
of DC3S) but wraps it in a full runtime safety
layer with constraint tightening, action shielding,
and governance certification. Stand-alone CP only
produces intervals; ORIUS consumes them, inflates
them according to degradation severity, and feeds the
result into a formal contract stack.
C. Safety Shielding and Control Barrier Functions
Shield synthesis computes a reactive strategy that
enforces a safety specification by overriding unsafe
controller actions [10]. Control Barrier Functions
(CBFs) [11] certify forward invariance of safe sets.
Both approaches typically require a known, accurate
state vector.

L. Fault Injection Methodology
Faults are injected into the SoC telemetry channel
at the adapter level, before the observation reaches
the DC3S kernel:
a) Dropout.:With probability pdrop = 0.15, the
SoC reading is replaced with NaN. The adapter’s
parse_observation method detects the NaN and
substitutes the last valid reading, marking the obser-
vation as degraded.
b) Stale sensor.:The SoC reading is frozen at its
value as oftfreeze, which is drawn uniformly from the
episode timeline. The freeze lasts for 5–15 intervals
(50–150 minutes).
c) Nominal.:No faults are applied; the SoC
telemetry matches the true state (up to model noise).

XII. Limitations and Future Work
A. Limitations
a) Three-domain scope.:This paper validates
ORIUS on three CPS domains. While the kernel is
designed to be universal, the empirical evidence in
the active repo-tracked program covers only battery
energy storage, autonomous vehicles, and healthcare
monitoring. The universality claim is therefore archi-
tectural and contract-based rather than a claim of
broad all-domain empirical closure.
b) AV denominator and relative-gap weakness.:
The all-zip grouped nuPlan AV row reaches empirical
ORIUS TSVR = 0.000163on 1,531,104 replay steps,
inside the explicit10−3runtime gate but not equal
to zero. The relative-gap shift table remains inwatch
status, and relative gap under degraded telemetry
is the hardest cell. The AV result should be read
as a runtime-contract replay closure, not as closed-
loop road deployment or full autonomous-driving field
closure.
c) Conformal coverage assumptions.:The finite-
sample coverage guarantee of split conformal pre-
diction holdsmarginally(averaged over the cali-
bration set) but notconditionally(for every indi-
vidual input). The shift-aware mechanism provides
robustness against subgroup-level violations but does
not eliminate them entirely. Per-input guarantees
would require conditional conformal methods not yet
integrated.
d) Shift guarantees are bounded.:The shift-aware
adaptive mechanism (Section VII) compensates for
distribution drift within the observed rate during
validation (A6). It hasnotbeen evaluated under
intentionally adversarial telemetry attacks, sudden-
onset covariate shift, or concept drift that violates
the bounded-rate assumption. Guarantees degrade
gracefully but may fail silently under pathological
shift patterns.
e) Fallback action conservatism.:Assumption
A4 requires that the fallback action is safe for all
states. In many domains, designing a universally
safe fallback is straightforward (eg.zero dispatch
for battery, maximum braking for AV). In domains
with coupled dynamics, identifying a universally safe
fallback may be non-trivial.
f) Single-sensor pipeline.:The current detect
stage focuses on single-channel telemetry faults. Cross-
sensor consistency checks (eg.lidar–camera fusion
disagreement) are not yet implemented in the adapter
interface.
g) Computational cost.:The bounded AV replay
run is not a real-time deployment benchmark. For
real-time operation at 10 Hz, the per-step budget is
100 ms, and the current Python reporting path would
still need compiled inference, batching, and transport
integration before it could support a live vehicle loop.
h) Cross-domain transfer scope.:The adapter
interface enables structural reuse across domains, but
functional equivalence (whether the same hyperpa-
rameters, calibration windows, and inflation schedules
are optimal) has not been demonstrated beyond the
active Battery, AV, and Healthcare rows. Adapting
to a new domain still requires implementing the six

=== PHYSICAL PAGE 29 ===
29
typed adapter methods and conducting a domain-
specific calibration run.
i) Governance and security ablations.:The run-
time contract can also fail through provenance and
governance channels rather than through prediction
error. Table 8.1 records the fail-closed ablations
used to keep these channels visible: missing model
hashes, bad certificate signatures, stale or replayed
device identity, invalid fallback actions, and corrupted
compact release witnesses.
j) Falsification boundaries.:ORIUS is strongest
when it states not only what passes, but also what
would falsify the current bounded claim. Table 8.2
lists the main ways the present evidence package
could fail and the corresponding demotion or rerun
obligation.
B. Future Work
1) Additional domains: Extend validation only
after a new domain is admitted into the active
program with matching replay, calibration, run-
time, and governance surfaces.
2) Stronger predictors: Replace LightGBM with
transformer-based trajectory predictors and
larger nuPlan denominators to stress the AV
row.
3) Conditional coverage: Integrate conditional
conformal prediction methods (eg.locally-
adaptive intervals) for per-input guarantees.
4) Multi-sensor fusion: Extend the detect stage
to perform cross-sensor consistency checks using
the adapter interface.
5) Real-time deployment: Optimise the runtime
for sub-100 ms latency via compiled inference
and hardware acceleration.
6) Formal verification: Apply model-checking or
SMT-based verification to the kernel contracts
for certified correctness beyond testing.
7) Adversarial robustness: Evaluate ORIUS un-
der intentionally adversarial telemetry attacks
beyond the fault-injection families tested here.

kernel—Detect,Calibrate,Constrain,Shield,Certify—
that closes the Observation–Action Safety Gap for
any upstream controller through typed contracts and
domain-agnostic governance.

Appendix H
Reliability Audit Protocol
The reliability audit stratifies the runtime behaviour into five OQE-weight bins and verifies that the
RAC-Cert inflation responds proportionally to degradation. For each bin, the audit checks:
1)Monotone interval width: lowerwt bins produce wider intervals.
2)Monotone intervention rate: lowerwt bins show higher IR.
3)Coverage maintenance: all bins maintain coverage≥1−α.
4)No over-conservatism: the highestwt bin does not inflate beyond the base conformal interval.
Both domains pass all four audit checks. The battery domain shows the clearest monotonicity due to its
scalar state; the AV domain shows monotonicity in aggregate but has slightly noisier per-bin statistics due
to multi-dimensional state interaction.
