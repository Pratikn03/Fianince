  From Human Markets to Machine Markets:
 Feedback Stability, Auditable Memory, and Financial Constraints

                    Pratik Niroula∗ and                 Prashant Niroula†

                                         October 8, 2026


                                              Abstract

      We study what a controlled aggregate price experiment can reveal about adaptive
      memory, and which financially specified resources make that inference possible. For a
      known symmetric single-versus-split cohort pair, total executed probing quantity has
      necessary and sufficient order δ −2 under Gaussian observation noise. A fixed lifetime
      quantity budget cannot be overcome by waiting, whereas an average-power budget
      yields horizon order δ −4 . Positive price-response gains connect this distinction to an
      explicit monetary output and cost allowance. These are conditional experiment re-
      sults, distinct from public participant attribution or operational solvency guarantees.
      Empirically, reconstruction of the original 768-unit market panel shows pronounced
      flow-timing and filter sensitivity; raw-return date intervals include zero and maintained
      covariance restrictions reject. Replayed financial simulations separate model inventory-
      limit events from defaults and expose nominal/effective persistence differences. The
      resulting evidence supports precise information and calibration requirements, while
      leaving causal learning attribution and real-market safety validation unresolved.


Keywords: Adaptive learning, algorithmic trading, financial stability, market microstruc-
ture, system identification, non-normal dynamics, Kreiss constant.

1     Introduction
Electronic execution and market making raise a financial identification question: what can
aggregate price and flow observations reveal about trading rules, and which financial con-
straints govern experiments intended to learn those rules? Microstructure research docu-
ments the interaction of trades, quotes and automated liquidity provision (Hasbrouck, 1991;
Menkveld, 2013; Budish, Cramton, and Shim, 2015). We study a declared linear feedback
economy and its information/resource contracts, then test whether observable measurement
diagnostics support the corresponding interpretation.
     When trading algorithms condition their order submission and quote-placement decisions
on recent transaction prices, prevailing order book imbalances, and trade innovations, the
price-formation process ceases to be an open-loop mechanism driven purely by exogenous
informational updates regarding fundamentals. Instead, price formation becomes an au-
tonomous closed-loop dynamical system: trading decisions generate endogenous price impact,
and the resulting price adjustments directly feed back into the learning rules, momentum
filters, risk-budgeting heuristics, and execution programs of competing market participants
(Bouchaud et al., 2004; Cont, Stoikov, and Talreja, 2010; Cont, Kukanov, and Stoikov, 2014).
     This closed-loop structural reality was dramatically underscored by major episodic dis-
ruptions in electronic markets over the past decade and a half. While disruptions such as
the May 6, 2010 Flash Crash in the E-mini S&P 500 and the October 15, 2014 Treasury
flash rally provide compelling historical motivation for analyzing how algorithmic feedback
can rapidly deplete liquidity, direct empirical investigation of these episodes is constrained
by the non-public nature of regulatory account registries. Modern electronic cryptocurrency
order books provide an ideal, transparent empirical testing ground. Electronic cryptocur-
rency venues provide public trade and book data, but these records do not identify the
fraction of activity generated by adaptive algorithms. The supplied samples have the clocks
and coverage described below; they do not establish universal nanosecond observation or
the absence of off-exchange execution. We therefore exploit high-frequency cryptocurrency
order books (complemented by a benchmark sample of regulated NASDAQ Nordic equities)
as an empirical testing ground for our broader theoretical framework.
     These structural phenomena confront financial economists, central banks, and market
regulators with an urgent, foundational question:

       Can observable market data identify destabilizing adaptive feedback, when does
           that feedback become financially dangerous, and what information is
                   fundamentally missing from public surveillance data?

    In this paper, we address this question directly by integrating realization-theoretic sys-

central advance; general unknown-mixture recovery and adaptive probing remain outside
the theorem.

be substituted for the controlled information contract.

1.1    Related Literature
Our work bridges four major strands of literature across financial economics, market mi-
crostructure, systemic risk, and dynamical systems theory.

High-Frequency Trading and Market Microstructure. Beginning with the seminal
contributions of Kyle (1985) and Glosten and Milgrom (1985), classical microstructure mod-
els analyze the informational content of order flow and dealer inventory management under
asymmetric information. Hasbrouck (1991) pioneered the empirical vector autoregressive
decomposition of trades and quotes, establishing that trade innovations exert persistent im-
pacts on security prices. Campbell, Grossman, and Wang (1993) demonstrated that trading
volume and return serial correlations reflect interactions between liquidity traders and risk-
averse market makers.
    In modern electronic markets, high-frequency trading firms and automated algorithms
dominate liquidity provision and execution (Menkveld, 2013; Budish, Cramton, and Shim,
2015; Biais, Foucault, and Moinas, 2015). While Hendershott, Jones, and Menkveld (2011)
document that algorithmic trading improves liquidity and narrows bid-ask spreads during
normal trading periods, Budish, Cramton, and Shim (2015) show that continuous double
auctions generate socially wasteful latency arms races, and Biais, Foucault, and Moinas
(2015) demonstrate that algorithmic speed advantages can lead to adverse selection spirals.
We advance this literature by formalizing the closed loop between algorithmic learning rules
and endogenous price impact, establishing fundamental realization-theoretic bounds on what
econometricians can infer from public ticker feeds.

Price Impact and Limit Order Book Dynamics. An extensive literature studies price
impact, signed-flow memory and quote imbalance (Bouchaud et al., 2004; Cont, Kukanov,

                                              3
Niroula and Niroula                                          From Human Markets to Machine Markets



and Stoikov, 2014). Our long-run regression diagnostics retain these as competing explana-
tions. Lag controls and observable kernel signs do not identify parent orders, learner policies
or contemporaneous structural shocks.

Intermediary Solvency, Funding Liquidity, and Systemic Risk. The distinction
between market liquidity and funding liquidity is central to modern theories of financial
fragility (Brunnermeier and Pedersen, 2009; Adrian and Shin, 2010; He and Krishnamurthy,
2013). When leveraged market makers suffer balance-sheet drawdowns, risk-management
constraints (such as Value-at-Risk limits or regulatory margin requirements) force them to
withdraw quotes, widening spreads and amplifying price dislocations (Greenwood, Landier,
and Thesmar, 2015; Eisenberg and Noe, 2001). Our analysis demonstrates that non-normal
transient amplification can trigger intermediary insolvency even when the underlying price-
formation process is spectrally stable (ρ(J) < 1).

Non-Normal Dynamics and the Kreiss Matrix Theorem. Non-normal transients
and Kreiss bounds are established mathematical tools (Kreiss, 1962; Spijker, 1991; Trefethen
and Embree, 2005). Their application to financial bubbles also precedes this paper: Sornette
et al. (2023) links subcritical financial dynamics to non-normal networks and the Kreiss
constant. We therefore do not claim first application to finance. Our proposed contribution
connects a particular memory-resolution experiment to executed-quantity and monetary-
output resources; the underlying norm and testing tools remain established.

1.2    Empirical Research Design and Setting
We retain the original multi-year panel, recorder timing/filter comparison, joint cross-venue
VAR, liquidity-state regressions, supplied equity representation, financial simulations and
forecast diagnostics. Each has its own clock, sample and inference assumptions. Existing raw
files and outputs are reconstructed before interpreting mechanisms. The paper distinguishes
an observed coefficient, a maintained-model test, a constructed financial scenario and a
controlled identification theorem. Data/provider and calibration boundaries accompany the
results; they are not inferred from a successful numerical reproduction.
     Theoretical identification and resource bounds appear first, followed by measurement
construction and reconstructed empirical studies. The final section gives the finite-window
input/output gate and its operational limitations.


2     Theoretical Model and Identification Boundaries
2.1    Microfoundations of Closed-Loop Price Formation
We formalize an electronic financial market sampled at discrete uniform time intervals
t ∈ {0, 1, 2, . . . }, where time step ∆ represents the operational sampling frequency (e.g.,
1 second). Let pt denote the price level in monetary units of a traded asset, and let p∗t
denote the unobserved fundamental value, which evolves as a martingale:

                             p∗t+1 = p∗t + ξt+1 ,       ξt+1 ∼ i.i.d.(0, σξ2 ).                    (3)

We define the pricing error (displacement from fundamental) as xt ≡ pt − p∗t .

tinuous auction framework of Kyle (1985) adapted to discrete order books (Glosten and
Milgrom, 1985; Hasbrouck, 1991; Bouchaud et al., 2004), competitive dealers absorb net
incoming order flow by posting aggregate limit order depth Dt > 0. Total incoming flow
combines algorithmic demand ut and exogenous liquidity noise εt+1 ∼ i.i.d.(0, σε2 ), counter-
balanced by fundamentalist arbitrageurs submitting demand −φxt .

Remark 2.4 (Scope of the Identification Result). The result concerns the stated model and
observation contract. Cohorts sharing an exact rate enter the price response through their
merged weight; this does not determine participant identities or wealth allocations. Nearby
rates can be distinguishable with sufficient information. The δ −4 statement is an asymptotic
lower bound on the declared uniformly stable family, with constants depending on that
family; it does not establish a numerical “160,000 times longer” comparison to a unit rate
change outside the local experiment. No matching general unknown-mixture upper bound
or universal market-surveillance rate is asserted here.

2.5    Financial Intermediary Solvency and Non-Normal Transient
       Amplification
We now confront the fundamental divergence between price stability and financial intermedi-
ary solvency. In standard economic policy models, market stability is evaluated exclusively
through the spectral radius ρ(J) < 1. We demonstrate that this criterion is dangerously
incomplete.

The Non-Normal Market Jacobian. Consider the closed-loop Jacobian matrix J from
(14). In financial markets, price impact and memory adaptation are asymmetric: order
flow directly affects prices through contemporaneous impact κ, while price innovations affect
memory through learning rate η. As a result, JJ ⊤ − J ⊤ J ̸= 0: the closed-loop market Jaco-
bian J is generically non-normal under asymmetric price-impact and learning parameters
(except on a measure-zero algebraic subvariety). While normal matrices have orthogonal
eigenvectors that diagonalize under unitary transformations, non-normal matrices have non-
orthogonal (nearly parallel) eigenvectors. Consequently, even when all eigenvalues are strictly
inside the unit circle (|λi (J)| ≤ ρ(J) < 1), matrix powers J t can undergo massive transient
amplification before decaying asymptotically as t → ∞ (Trefethen and Embree, 2005).

3.3    Covariance Restrictions and the Limits of Heteroskedastic
       Identification
For a stationary VAR, Γ0 = JΓ0 J ⊤ + Σ and Γk = J k Γ0 . Matching three contemporaneous
and eight lagged covariance entries gives 11 moments and seven transition/noise parameters.
Our diagnostic uses a full stable transition and positive-definite innovation parameterization,
segment-aware moment HAC and six starts per declared case. All 24 cases (three assets, two
centering rules and four bandwidths) reject their maintained restrictions under their stated
asymptotic reference. For global centering and bandwidth ten bars, J statistics are 41.4063,
28.3094 and 12.1175 for BTC, ETH and ADA. These rejections do not validate the fitted
economic model. The earlier restricted-fit statistic 73 concerns a different parameter class
and is retained only in the historical ledger.
    Contemporaneous heteroskedastic identification additionally requires invariant structural
coefficients, valid innovations, orthogonal structural shocks and a defended regime partition.
Nonproportional variance shifts alone do not supply all of these. In the two-equation system,
exchanging shock labels maps (λ, c) to (1/c, 1/λ) while preserving each covariance after vari-
ance rescaling. Sign or economic exclusions can distinguish some branches, but the current
source’s single bounded numerical root is not an identification proof. Its forward-flow pair-
ing and endogenous volatility grouping do not establish the required assumptions. Thus the
old negative coefficient is not evidence that market makers provide identified instantaneous
buffering. The exact branch-equivalence witness and all covariance sensitivities are retained.

7     Regulatory Policy Implications and Conclusion
7.1    Pre-Trade Controls and the Scope of the Proposed Bound
Existing market-access rules already include pre-trade controls. SEC Rule 15c3-5 requires
controls against orders exceeding appropriate credit/capital thresholds and erroneous orders;
the SEC staff FAQ discusses their application to quotes and automated activity (SEC, 2010;
SEC Staff, 2017). The present mathematical bound is a conditional design input. Its deriva-
tion does not establish regulatory compliance, improved welfare or an implemented exchange
safety mechanism.

7.3    Market Design: Continuous Auctions vs. Frequent Batch Auc-
       tions
Our findings also speak directly to the structural debate on market design initiated by
Budish, Cramton, and Shim (2015), who propose replacing continuous double auctions with
Frequent Batch Auctions (FBAs) to eliminate latency arbitrage. Batch matching changes ex-
ecution timing and incentives; a feedback model can also be posed across successive batches.
This paper does not estimate its comparative economic effect. The finite-window quantity
bound can be examined under either matching design when its input/output and account-
ing assumptions hold. Comparative welfare, execution feasibility and effectiveness across

7.4    Information Missing from Public Market Feeds
Our realization-theoretic theorems prove that public market data suffer from fundamental
blind spots: (i) within the reset price-response contract, cohorts sharing the same rate en-
ter through a merged weight (Theorem 2.3); (ii) the scalar Gaussian experiment yields a
finite-window abstention bound near its unit-root boundary (Theorem 2.7); and (iii) stable
pricing eigenvalues do not guarantee dealer solvency under non-normal transient magnifica-
tion (Theorem 2.9). Mitigating systemic fragility requires complementing public surveillance
with audited memory buffers, pre-trade capital checks, and synchronized audit trails.


7.5    Conclusion
We establish a resource distinction within a precisely defined memory-resolution experiment:
lifetime executed quantity of order δ −2 is necessary and sufficient for a known symmetric pair,
while average power gives horizon order δ −4 . A fixed monetary-output and accumulated-cost
allowance bounds quantity; more waiting alone cannot overcome that lifetime resource limit.
The empirical reconstruction shows strong timing/filter sensitivity and maintained-model
rejection, rather than participant-level learning attribution. Existing simulations distinguish
capacity events, defaults and effective dynamics. Real-market financial calibration and inde-
pendent specialist assessment remain necessary before publication and operational claims.
