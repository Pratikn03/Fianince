\section{The identification boundary}\label{sec:ident}

A central question in algorithmic market surveillance is whether an observer of executions and prices can identify the individual strategies active in the market.

\begin{theorem}[Merged rate--weight equivalence]\label{thm:merged}
Let $\kappa>0$ and $a\in[0,1)$ be known. Use zero initial states and a common probe contract, and observe the modeled price state $x_t$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{Equivalence class.} Two economies $\mathcal E^A=\{(q_i^A,\eta_i^A)\}$ and $\mathcal E^B=\{(q_j^B,\eta_j^B)\}$ produce identical price responses for every probe sequence if and only if their merged measures coincide:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:merged}
\sum_{i:\eta_i^A=\eta} q_i^A\eta \;=\; \sum_{j:\eta_j^B=\eta} q_j^B\eta\qquad\forall\eta\in(0,1].
\end{equation}
Write $a'=a+\kappa M(\{0\})$, so that cohorts with $\eta=1$ (rate $0$) act as part of the anchor. For $a'\neq0$ the minimal realization has dimension $1+|\mathrm{supp}(M)\cap(0,1)|$, counting distinct positive rates with nonzero merged weight after signed cancellation; for $a'=0$ the price carries no state of its own and the dimension is $|\mathrm{supp}(M)\cap(0,1)|$. A cohort with $\eta=1$ merges into the anchor $a$ without adding a pole.
\item \emph{Separation complexity under average power.} Consider testing a single cohort with rate $\bar\eta$ against two cohorts with rates $\bar\eta\pm\delta$ and the same total weight, on a compact family that is uniformly Schur stable with all rates in $[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]$ for some $\epsilon>0$. Observe $\tilde x_t=x_t+\nu_t$ with $\nu_t\sim N(0,\sigma_\nu^2)$ and use deterministic probes with $\frac1T\sum_{t<T}v_t^2\le P$. Any test with worst-case error at most $\alpha\in(0,1/4)$ needs
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Tpower}
T\;\ge\;\frac{4\sigma_\nu^2(1-2\alpha)^2}{C_{\mathcal F}^2P\,\delta^4}=\Omega(\delta^{-4}),
\end{equation}
where $C_{\mathcal F}$ depends only on the family, through $\epsilon$, the bound on $|H|$ implied by uniform stability, and the total weight $q$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{In words.} If two trading desks update on prices with the same memory, no amount of price data can tell them apart or say how much each trades: prices reveal only how much trading happens at each memory speed. Desks with different memories can be told apart in principle, and the rest of the paper prices how hard that is.


The memory rates are poles of $G$ and \emph{zeros} of $H$, not closed-loop poles. This has a practical consequence we verify in Section~\ref{sec:mixtures}: pole-fitting methods applied directly to the price impulse response recover closed-loop eigenvalues ($0.4256$ and $0.8224$ for $K=1$, $\eta=0.3$, $a=0.5$, $\kappa=0.8$, $q=0.2$) and miss the memory rate $0.70$.

\begin{remark}[Scope]\label{rem:scope}
The result concerns the stated model and observation contract. Cohorts sharing an exact rate enter prices through their merged weight; this does not determine identities or wealth allocations. Nearby rates can be distinguishable with enough information. The $\delta^{-4}$ statement in (ii) is a lower bound on a declared family, with family-dependent constants. Individual algorithms may choose their rates strategically, but any unilateral change maps into the same observer-facing merged measure, which reinforces rather than circumvents the boundary.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Conditional endogenous-pricing invariance]\label{prop:endogenous_kappa}
Let $\kappa=\mathcal K(\Gamma_M(\kappa))$ on a common admissible interval, with identical innovation laws, initial-state conventions and other primitives across economies. Here $\Gamma_M(\kappa)$ is the specified covariance record at a fixed impact and $\mathcal K$ is a maintained dealer rule, not a derived general equilibrium.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If equal merged measures imply the same map $\Gamma_M$ and the fixed point is unique (or a common selection rule depends only on that map), equal merged measures give equal selected impacts.
\item Suppose $\mathcal K$ is $L_K$-Lipschitz, each covariance map is $L_\Gamma$-Lipschitz in impact, $L_KL_\Gamma<1$, and $\sup_\kappa\|\Gamma_A(\kappa)-\Gamma_B(\kappa)\|\le\epsilon$. For existing fixed points,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kappa_closeness}
 |\kappa_A-\kappa_B|\le\frac{L_K\epsilon}{1-L_KL_\Gamma}.
\end{equation}
Thus a covariance discrepancy of order $(\Delta\eta)^{2K-1}$ preserves that order only under these uniform conditions.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Limits of the pricing extension.} Anonymous flow alone implies neither a covariance-only dealer objective nor uniqueness or continuity of its equilibrium. Multiple equilibria, threshold quote changes, private information and inventory-dependent rules are outside this proposition; for the question of invariance, Theorem~\ref{thm:eqinv} below covers them. Preserving a price-record testing bound additionally requires a uniformly Lipschitz observation law with the same nondegenerate noise; adding anonymous flow is a different statistical experiment and requires its own divergence bound. We therefore make no unconditional claim that endogenous pricing or additional aggregate-flow observations preserve the price-only lower bound.

\section{Equilibrium foundations}\label{sec:equilibrium}

The identification results take the price impact $\kappa$ and the persistence $a$ of pricing errors as given, and nobody in the model optimizes. This section derives both from dealers who dislike holding inventory. It then shows four things:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item the equilibrium price is the paper's closed loop with effective parameters, so the identification boundary and the rates hold in equilibrium, and positive feedback creates an explicit fragility threshold;
\item a strategic insider cannot change it;
\item misjudging the aggregate response creates rents of first order;
\item targeted actions still need group-level information.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{An inventory equilibrium.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Fundamental and price.} The fundamental $F_t$ is a public random walk with innovation variance $\sigma_F^2$, and the price is $p_t=F_t+x_t$.
\item \emph{Cohorts.} They trade $u_{i,t+1}=r_iu_{i,t}+w_ix_t$, the paper's exponential memory with $w_i=q_i\eta_i$ and $r_i=1-\eta_i$.
\item \emph{Exogenous flow.} $\xi_t\sim N(0,\sigma_\xi^2)$, i.i.d., stands for noise trades or the observer's probe. Customer flow is $y_t=\sum_iu_{i,t}+\xi_t$.
\item \emph{Dealers.} Competitive dealers absorb the flow and lay off a share $1-\bar a$ of their inventory each period, so $H_t=\bar aH_{t-1}-y_t$. Each overlapping generation of dealers has CARA risk aversion $A$ and holds $H_t$ to the next date: $H_t=(E_tp_{t+1}-p_t)/(AV)$, with $V=\mathrm{Var}_t(p_{t+1})$.
\end{itemize}
We study stationary equilibria in which $x_t$ is linear in the dealers' inventory and the cohorts' next trades. Write $G(D)=\sum_iw_i/(D-r_i)=\int dM(r)/(D-r)$ for the Stieltjes transform of the merged measure.

\begin{theorem}[Price impact and persistence in an inventory equilibrium]\label{thm:inventory}
Consider stationary linear equilibria of the model above.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Characterization.} The price law
\[
 x_t=-\kappa H_t+\kappa\sum_i\frac{u_{i,t+1}}{D-r_i}
\]
with $D\ne0$ and $D\ne r_i$, is an equilibrium exactly when the induced dynamics are stable and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inventory_eq}
 D=1-\kappa\,G(D),\qquad \kappa(D-\bar a)=A\,V\,D,\qquad V=\sigma_F^2+\frac{\kappa^2\sigma_\xi^2}{D^2}.
\end{equation}
An unexpected unit of flow moves the price by $\kappa/D$.
\item \emph{No feedback.} Then $D=1$ and the price law is exactly the paper's $x_t=\bar a\,x_{t-1}+\kappa\,y_t$. The persistence of pricing errors is the dealers' carry share, $a=\bar a$, and the impact $\kappa$ solves $\kappa(1-\bar a)=A(\sigma_F^2+\kappa^2\sigma_\xi^2)$. An equilibrium exists if and only if $\Delta=(1-\bar a)^2-4A^2\sigma_F^2\sigma_\xi^2\ge0$. When $\Delta>0$ there are two, $\kappa_\pm=\bigl((1-\bar a)\pm\sqrt\Delta\bigr)/(2A\sigma_\xi^2)$; the low-impact one $\kappa_-\approx A\sigma_F^2/(1-\bar a)$ is the price of dealer inventory risk.
\item \emph{Invariance.} The coefficients $(\kappa,D,V)$, and hence the price process, depend on the cohorts only through $G$, and so only through the merged measure $M$. This is Theorem~\ref{thm:eqinv} in equilibrium form.
\item \emph{Small feedback.} Let $\Delta>0$, fix $\delta>0$ with $\max_ir_i<1-\delta$, and let $\epsilon=\sup_{|D-1|\le\delta}(|G(D)|+|G'(D)|)$ be small. Then there is a unique equilibrium near $(\kappa_-,1)$. With $G(1)=\sum_iq_i$, the aggregate feedback intensity,
\[
 D=1-\kappa_-G(1)+o(\epsilon),\qquad \kappa=\kappa_-+\frac{\kappa_-^2(\bar a+2A\kappa_-\sigma_\xi^2)}{\sqrt\Delta}\,G(1)+o(\epsilon).
\]
Positive feedback raises the impact $\kappa$, and it raises the impact of an unexpected flow shock, $\kappa/D$, by more.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{In words.} Dealers set the price impact and the persistence of mispricing. Impact is what they charge for carrying inventory risk; persistence is how slowly they lay that inventory off. Without feedback traders, this equilibrium is exactly the closed loop the paper starts from. Feedback traders enter only through their merged measure, as Theorem~\ref{thm:eqinv} requires. Positive feedback raises price impact, and dealers price their predictable future trades in advance (the terms in $u_{i,t+1}$). Feedback trading also makes liquidity fragile, not only more expensive: beyond a threshold in dealer risk aversion that falls with the feedback intensity, no equilibrium exists (Theorem~\ref{thm:fragility}).

\paragraph{Machine check.} \nolinkurl{Paper1InventoryEquilibrium.lean} checks the algebraic core of part (i):
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item the dealers' first-order condition holds in every state;
\item the next price loads on the next flow shock with coefficient $\kappa/D$;
\item the no-feedback existence condition.
\end{itemize}
Parts (iii) and (iv) are proved on paper.

\paragraph{The identification results hold in equilibrium.} With feedback, dealers price the cohorts' predictable trades in advance, so the response of prices to flow is no longer the reduced form of Section~\ref{sec:model} with the primitive $(a,\kappa)$. The next theorem shows that it is still that closed loop, exactly and path by path, with new parameters and the same memory rates.

\begin{theorem}[The equilibrium is the paper's closed loop]\label{thm:eqreduced}
Take an equilibrium of Theorem~\ref{thm:inventory} with $D\ne\bar a$, and set
\[
 a^*=\frac{\bar a}{D},\qquad \kappa^*=\frac{\kappa}{D},\qquad \tilde w_i=\frac{D-\bar a}{D-r_i}\,w_i .
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Reduced form, pathwise.} Let $\tilde u_{i,t}=\frac{D-\bar a}{D-r_i}u_{i,t}$. For every realization, every initial state and every $t$,
\[
 x_t=a^*x_{t-1}+\kappa^*\Bigl(\xi_t+\sum_i\tilde u_{i,t}\Bigr),\qquad \tilde u_{i,t+1}=r_i\tilde u_{i,t}+\tilde w_ix_t .
\]
So the price follows the closed loop \eqref{eq:price}--\eqref{eq:cohort} with persistence $a^*$, impact $\kappa^*$, exogenous flow $\xi$ in place of the probe, and cohorts with the same memory rates and merged weights $\tilde w_i$. Its transfer function from flow to price is \eqref{eq:H} with $(a^*,\kappa^*,\tilde w)$. The equilibrium is stable if and only if this closed loop is.
\item \emph{Zeros.} For $\bar a>0$, distinct rates in $(0,1)$ and nonzero weights, the memory rates are the transmission zeros of the equilibrium price response, and none cancels (Theorem~\ref{thm:minphase}(i) with the effective parameters).
\item \emph{Boundary.} The effective merged measure is $\tilde M=h\,M$, where $h(r)=(D-\bar a)/(D-r)$ is positive. The effective parameters determine the equilibrium ones: $D=1-\kappa^*\tilde M([0,1])/(1-a^*)$, $\bar a=a^*D$ and $\kappa=\kappa^*D$. So the merged measure remains exactly what equilibrium prices identify (Theorem~\ref{thm:merged}).
\item \emph{The rates survive.} Let $(a^*,\kappa^*,W,r_c,\Delta\eta)$ satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:kcohort} with $(a,\kappa)=(a^*,\kappa^*)$. Then the two $K$-cohort economies of that theorem are the price responses of two inventory equilibria with identical primitives $(A,\sigma_F^2,\sigma_\xi^2,\bar a)$ and identical $(\kappa,D)$:
 \begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
 \item $D=1-\kappa^*W/(1-a^*)$, which exceeds every rate;
 \item $\bar a=a^*D$ and $\kappa=\kappa^*D$;
 \item any $\sigma_F^2,\sigma_\xi^2>0$, with $A=\kappa^*D(1-a^*)/(\sigma_F^2+\kappa^{*2}\sigma_\xi^2)$;
 \item cohort weights $w_i=\tilde w_i(D-r_i)/(D-\bar a)>0$.
 \end{itemize}
 Hence an observer of equilibrium prices who knows the primitives needs $N\ge c\,\sigma^2(\Delta\eta)^{-(4K-2)}$ experiments. Theorem~\ref{prop:esprit} applies to the effective parameters, and the true weights follow from the effective ones by a Lipschitz map. So $N^*\asymp\sigma^2(\Delta\eta)^{-(4K-2)}$ in equilibrium.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{In words.} Dealers' anticipation does not hide the cohorts and does not move them. It replaces the persistence and impact by $\bar a/D$ and $\kappa/D$, and it rescales each cohort's weight by $(D-\bar a)/(D-r_i)$. Because that factor rises with $r_i$, slow-memory cohorts weigh more in equilibrium prices. The memory rates are untouched.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item Every result of Sections~\ref{sec:ident}, \ref{sec:resources} and~\ref{sec:mixtures} that concerns the map from order flow to prices therefore holds in equilibrium: identification, sample complexity, passive observation, and probing costs computed from the price path. Its constants are evaluated at the effective parameters.
\item The lower-bound pair is not an artefact of the reduced form. Two economies with the same dealers, the same risk aversion and the same impact differ only in their cohorts, and they are as hard to tell apart as Theorem~\ref{thm:kcohort} says.
\item Part (i), the realization in part (iv) and the effective cohort recursion are machine-checked (\nolinkurl{Paper1EquilibriumReducedForm.lean}).
\item One assumption is new: the observer's probe is part of the exogenous flow that dealers price as noise. Dealers who recognized a probe would price it differently.
\end{itemize}

\begin{theorem}[Fragility: where liquidity disappears]\label{thm:fragility}
Let there be one feedback cohort with weight $w>0$ and memory rate $r\in(0,1)$, with $\bar a\in[0,1)$ and $\sigma_F,\sigma_\xi>0$. Call an equilibrium of Theorem~\ref{thm:inventory} with $\kappa>0$ and $D>0$ a positive-impact equilibrium. For $D\in(m,1)$ with $m=\max(\bar a,r)$, let
\[
 \kappa_w(D)=\frac{(1-D)(D-r)}{w},\qquad \Psi_w(D)=\frac{\kappa_w(D)\,(D-\bar a)\,D}{\sigma_F^2D^2+\kappa_w(D)^2\sigma_\xi^2},\qquad A^*(w)=\max_{D\in(m,1)}\Psi_w(D).
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Threshold.}
 \begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
 \item The positive-impact equilibria are exactly the pairs $(\kappa_w(D),D)$ with $D\in(m,1)$ and $\Psi_w(D)=A$, and every one of them is stable.
 \item A positive-impact equilibrium exists if and only if $A\le A^*(w)$.
 \item For $A<A^*(w)$ there are at least two; for $A>A^*(w)$ there are none.
 \end{itemize}
\item \emph{Monotonicity.} $A^*(w)$ is strictly decreasing in $w$. As $w\downarrow0$ it tends to $A^*_0=(1-\bar a)/(2\sigma_F\sigma_\xi)$, the threshold of Theorem~\ref{thm:inventory}(ii).
\item \emph{Bounds and rate.} For every $w>0$,
\[
 A^*(w)<\min\Bigl\{A^*_0,\ \frac{\Phi^*}{\sigma_F^2\,w}\Bigr\},
\]
 where
\[
 \Phi^*=\max_{D\in[m,1]}\frac{(1-D)(D-r)(D-\bar a)}{D}\le\frac{(1-\bar a)(1-r)^2}{4},
\]
 and $w\,A^*(w)\to\Phi^*/\sigma_F^2$ as $w\to\infty$. In terms of the feedback intensity $G(1)=w/(1-r)$, strong feedback gives $A^*\approx\Phi^*/(\sigma_F^2(1-r)G(1))$, and always $A^*<(1-\bar a)(1-r)/(4\sigma_F^2G(1))$.
\item \emph{Several cohorts.} Let all weights be positive.
 \begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
 \item The positive-impact equilibria with $D>\max_ir_i$ are exactly the pairs $((1-D)/G(D),D)$ with $D\in(\max(\bar a,\max_ir_i),1)$ at which the analogue of $\Psi_w$, with $\kappa=(1-D)/G(D)$, equals $A$.
 \item Every such equilibrium has
 \[
  A<\min\Bigl\{A^*_0,\ \frac{(1-\sqrt{\bar a})^2}{\sigma_F^2\,G(1)}\Bigr\},
 \]
 whatever the number of cohorts and their rates.
 \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{In words.} With positive feedback, dealers can make a market only if their dislike of risk is below a threshold, and the threshold falls as feedback grows: for strong feedback it is inversely proportional to the feedback intensity. Past the threshold, no price impact is consistent with dealers' optimization. Below it, every equilibrium has stable prices. Liquidity does not erode through growing instability; it disappears when the two equilibria merge, at a point that stable prices give no warning of. At the calibration of Supplement~\ref{app:eqinv} ($\bar a=0.6$, $r=0.7$, $\sigma_F^2=1$, $\sigma_\xi^2=0.25$), $A^*$ is 0.40 without feedback, 0.25 at $G(1)=\tfrac1{15}$ and 0.034 at $G(1)=\tfrac23$, and $w\,A^*$ approaches $\Phi^*=0.00688$. The necessary conditions behind parts (i) and (iii) are machine-checked (\nolinkurl{Paper1EquilibriumReducedForm.lean}). With several cohorts, part (iv) gives a threshold that depends on the population only through its total feedback intensity $G(1)$. At the same calibration, numerically, the exact thresholds for two and three cohorts lie between 28\% and 86\% of that bound, and the equilibria on the threshold curve are stable. Stability with several cohorts is checked numerically, not proved.



\begin{theorem}[Equilibrium invariance]\label{thm:eqinv}
Consider a market with the following participants and rules.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A value $v$ and noise trades $z_t$.
\item An insider who submits $x_t=X_t(v,\mathcal H_{t-1})$.
\item A dealer who sets $p_t=P_t(\mathcal H_{t-1},y_t)$ from the public history $\mathcal H_{t-1}$ and the total order flow $y_t=x_t+z_t+f_t$.
\item Feedback cohorts whose memory states update on a public price statistic $s_t=S_t(p_0,\ldots,p_t)$ by $m_{i,t+1}=r_im_{i,t}+s_t$ from $m_{i,0}=0$, with $r_i=1-\eta_i$ and aggregate flow $f_t=\sum_iw_im_{i,t}$, $w_i=q_i\eta_i$.
\end{itemize}
The rules $X$, $P$ and $S$ are arbitrary causal maps. This covers Kyle, inventory-averse, learning and misspecified dealers, and any insider strategy.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),itemsep=1pt]
\item $f_t=\sum_{m<t}k_m\,s_{t-1-m}$, with $k_m=\sum_iw_ir_i^m$, the moments of the merged measure $M$.
\item Two cohort configurations produce the same aggregate flow for every price path on dates $t\le T$ if and only if $k_m=k'_m$ for all $m<T$. On an unbounded horizon this holds if and only if their merged measures coincide.
\item If $k_m=k'_m$ for $m<T$, then for every strategy profile and every realization of $(v,z)$ the following coincide on $t\le T$: the price and order-flow paths, the insider's trades and realized profit, and the dealer's inventory. The two games therefore have the same strategy sets and the same payoff for every profile. They have the same equilibria, with the same outcomes, under any equilibrium concept defined from these payoffs and the public history.
\item Group-level quantities are not invariant. A Gauss and a Radau two-point configuration agree on $k_0,k_1,k_2$, so they are outcome-equivalent for $T=3$, while their largest cohorts carry weights $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac34$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{In words.} Whatever the insider and the dealer do, two markets whose trader groups have the same merged response produce the same prices, the same order flow and the same insider and dealer outcomes. So the part of the population that prices cannot identify (Theorem~\ref{thm:merged}) is worth nothing to anyone who trades on public information. It matters only for actions aimed at a particular group, such as halting it (Proposition~\ref{prop:target}). Parts (i)--(iv) are machine-checked pathwise for every causal insider, dealer and signal rule (\nolinkurl{Paper1EquilibriumInvariance.lean}). Supplement~\ref{app:eqinv} gives the proof, the unbounded-horizon statement, the extension to cohort-specific demand shocks and a numerical illustration.

\paragraph{A misjudged dealer and a targeted halt.} Supplement~\ref{app:eqinv} adds two results in the simplest linear market covered by Theorem~\ref{thm:eqinv}.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Rents.} A dealer that misjudges the aggregate feedback response by $\varepsilon$ hands an insider with a committed schedule a rent of exact order $\varepsilon$, or charges it one (Theorem~\ref{thm:rents}). Public data reveal the aggregate response at the ordinary rate. So the information that protects price formation is cheap to learn, while who the groups are stays expensive.
\item \emph{A targeted halt.} Two markets that agree in equilibrium to within the cube of the memory spacing can differ at order one after their largest group is halted (Theorem~\ref{thm:eqhalt}). Equilibrium sharpens the case for targeted information rather than removing it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Probing resources, memory resolution and money}\label{sec:resources}

Observation length alone cannot buy identification when the lifetime quantity of controlled executions is fixed. This section connects the merged-memory experiment to a financially specified input/output contract. Throughout, $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function.

\subsection{Quantity}

\begin{theorem}[Sharp probing-resource orders for a known symmetric pair]\label{thm:quantity}
Fix $0\le a<1$, $\kappa,q>0$, $S=a+\kappa q<1$ and $\eta\in(0,1)$. Compare a single cohort of weight $q$ and rate $\eta$ with two cohorts of weight $q/2$ and rates $\eta\pm\delta$, both in $[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]$. All parameters are known. The price receives executed quantity through $\kappa v_t$, starts from zero, has no process noise, and is observed with independent $N(0,\sigma^2)$ noise. Probes are deterministic and common to both alternatives. Let $d_T=(h^\delta_j-h^0_j)_{j<T}$ and $K_*=2\kappa^2q/[\epsilon^3(1-S)^2]$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item For every probe with $\sum_t|v_t|\le V$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:klV}
\KL(P_0^v\|P_\delta^v)\le\frac{K_*^2V^2\delta^4}{2\sigma^2},\qquad e_{\rm worst}\ge\frac12\Bigl(1-\frac{K_*V\delta^2}{2\sigma}\Bigr)_+ .
\end{equation}
\item The optimal worst-case error over these probes and all classifiers is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exacterr}
e^*_T(V)=\Phi\!\left(-\frac{V\|d_T\|_2}{2\sigma}\right),
\end{equation}
attained by a pulse at the first input and the likelihood-ratio test. Waiting adds information but $\|d_T\|_2\uparrow\|d_\infty\|_2<\infty$, so it cannot make the error arbitrarily small at fixed $V$. Error at most $\alpha<1/2$ requires $V\ge2\sigma(1-2\alpha)/(K_*\delta^2)$ independently of $T$.
\item The first two taps coincide and $d_2=-\kappa^2q\delta^2$. A single pulse of size
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pulse}
A=\frac{2\sigma\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)}{\kappa^2q\delta^2}
\end{equation}
and the third observation achieve error exactly $\alpha$. The necessary and sufficient quantity order is $\delta^{-2}$.
\item Under average power $\sum_{t<T}v_t^2\le PT$, the necessary horizon order is $\delta^{-4}$, attained by the pulse whenever $T\ge\max\{3,\lceil A^2/P\rceil\}$.
\item For a fixed monetary price exposure $\lambda\neq0$, additive reserve $L$ and cost envelope $c\sum|v_t|$, the sharp robust quantity allowance under budget $B>L$ is $V_B=(B-L)/(|\lambda|\kappa+c)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{In words.} To tell one desk from two desks whose memories differ slightly, the observer must trade against them, and the quantity it must trade grows like $1/\delta^2$: halving the difference quadruples the trading needed. Waiting longer after a fixed trade does not help, because the trade's echo in the price dies out.


The identity in (iii) follows by direct substitution, and code checks it at $\delta\in\{0.02,0.05,0.10\}$: the difference is $-1.2\times10^{-4}$, $-7.5\times10^{-4}$ and $-3.0\times10^{-3}$ at $\kappa=1$, $q=0.3$, and $-3.2\times10^{-4}$ at the calibration $\kappa=0.8$, $q=0.2$, $\delta=0.05$. The theorem does not solve estimation of an unknown mixture (Section~\ref{sec:mixtures}).

Figure~\ref{fig:impulse} shows why the problem is hard. The two economies have the same direct impact and the same first-lag response; they first differ at lag two, by an amount proportional to $\delta^2$, and the whole difference sequence scales with $\delta^2$. A test therefore needs the signal-to-noise ratio $V\|d\|/\sigma$ to grow like $\delta^{-2}$, which is the quantity order, and because the adverse mark of a probe grows with the square of its size, capital grows like $\delta^{-4}$.

\subsection{Execution cost}

A probe changes inventory as well as price. We therefore specify cash and execution timing. Price has units of currency per asset, and $\kappa$ currency per squared quantity.

\begin{lemma}[Fair execution]\label{lem:toeplitz}
For a quote schedule refreshed after the uncontrolled state update, execution at the average price of a linear book costs, relative to the constant fundamental mark,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cost}
C(v)=\tfrac12v^\top T_hv+c\|v\|_1,\qquad (T_h)_{ij}=h_{|i-j|}.
\end{equation}
In the positive stable economy $T_h$ is positive definite, so no nonzero round trip produces a deterministic gain.
\end{lemma}

Positivity here means that the response is a positive mixture of decaying exponentials (Lemma~\ref{lem:cm}), whose spectral density is positive. Positive, decreasing taps alone do not suffice: for $h=(1,0.9,0.1)$ the $3\times3$ matrix $T_h$ has determinant $-0.468$, so some round trip earns money. The conclusion concerns modelled execution cost only.

We verify the ledger identity \eqref{eq:cost} against an explicit cash account to $5.7\times10^{-13}$ on 1,000 random probes, and the smallest eigenvalue of $T_h$ over 1,000 random stable positive economies is $0.141$. A direct-only proxy $\sum\tfrac12\kappa v_t^2$ omits cross-time impact: for $v=(+1,-1)$ it gives $0.80$ against a true Toeplitz loss of $h_0-h_1=0.3488$.

\subsection{Capital supplied by a counterparty}

\begin{theorem}[Sharp robust quantity cap from cash and margin]\label{thm:cap}
Use the positive, stable, reset economy with constant fundamental price $P_*>0$ and no process noise. A designated dealer fills only nonnegative probes $v_t\ge0$ at the average price $P^0_{t+1}+\kappa v_t/2$ of a linear schedule, holds inventory $I_{t+1}=I_t-v_t$ and cash $K_{t+1}=K_t+v_t(P^0_{t+1}+\kappa v_t/2)-cv_t$, starts with equity $B>0$, and must satisfy $\mu|I_t|P_t\le E_t$ at every time. For the robust contract admitting every probe with $\sum_tv_t\le V$, the largest allowance guaranteeing margin is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:VB}
V_B=\frac{2B}{\beta+\sqrt{\beta^2+4(\mu+\tfrac12)\kappa B}},\qquad\beta=c+\mu P_*,
\end{equation}
equivalently $(\mu+\tfrac12)\kappa V^2+(c+\mu P_*)V\le B$. The cap holds across all positive cohort representations with $S<1$, without resolving individual rates. An observer that receives only noisy prices and executed quantities has best error $\Phi[-V_B\|d_T\|_2/(2\sigma)]$ within this allowance; fixed error $\alpha<1/2$ requires capital of order at least $\delta^{-4}$, and a single positive pulse attains that order.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{In words.} A probe moves the price against the account that makes it, so the margin it needs grows with the square of the probe. Because the probe grows like $1/\delta^2$, the capital grows like $1/\delta^4$: halving the difference multiplies the capital needed by 16.


Even with zero initial inventory, the probe creates a quadratic adverse mark that a fixed initial price exposure would miss. Figure~\ref{fig:knownpair} illustrates the experiment.

(b) The sharp robust quantity allowance \eqref{eq:VB}. (c) Capital for error $0.1$ with 200 observations. \begin{proposition}[Exact financial disclosure changes the experiment]\label{prop:disclosure}
In this noiseless-state account, exact disclosure of the third equity mark after a single positive pulse $0<A\le V_B$ distinguishes the known pair with zero error for every $\delta>0$.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{In words.} If the observer can see an account's exact equity, a single test trade settles the question, however close the two candidate markets are. The hard part of the problem is therefore not the market itself but what the observer is allowed to see.

Exact marked equity is extra information, not a free observation assumed by the price-only theorem. Real account marks have their own precision: with reports rounded to $0.01$, a guaranteed change requires a larger trade (Section~\ref{sec:disclosure}).

\subsection{Capital paid by the probe trader}

\begin{lemma}[Time-domain Toeplitz coercivity: capital order for funded probes]\label{thm:capital}
Use the same economy and execution law. The statistical observer receives only noisy prices and executed quantities. The probe trader starts with cash $B>0$ and zero inventory; a finite signed sequence $v$ changes inventory by $+v_t$ and cash by $K_{t+1}=K_t-v_t(P^0_{t+1}+\kappa v_t/2)-c|v_t|$, after which trading stops and the trader must remain solvent as the price returns to $P_*$. Put
\[
r=1-\epsilon(1-S)<1,\qquad m=\kappa\frac{1-r}{1+r}>0,\qquad K_*=\frac{2\kappa^2q}{\epsilon^3(1-S)^2}.
\]
Every solvent strategy satisfies $\KL(P_0^v\|P_\delta^v)\le K_*^2B\delta^4/(\sigma^2m)$, so error at most $\alpha<1/2$ requires
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Bmin}
B\;\ge\;\frac{2\sigma^2m(1-2\alpha)^2}{K_*^2\delta^4},
\end{equation}
without a quantity cap and at any horizon. Conversely, for any margin fraction $0<\mu\le1$, the pulse $A$ in \eqref{eq:pulse} achieves error $\alpha$ and remains margin-solvent at every later mark with capital $B_A=\tfrac{\kappa}{2}A^2+(c+\mu P_*)A$. Necessary and sufficient capital orders are $\delta^{-4}$.
\end{lemma}

Lemma~\ref{thm:capital} is an order statement: an elementary time-domain coercivity argument for Toeplitz forms that establishes the $\delta^{-4}$ rate before the exact frequency-domain optimization of Section~\ref{sec:account}. Its explicit constant is very conservative, because it scales as $m/K_*^2\propto\epsilon^7(1-S)^5$: at the calibrated parameters it is $4.9\times10^{-4}$ par units at $\delta=0.05$ with $\epsilon=0.35$, five orders of magnitude below any feasible design. We therefore state it as a lemma; Section~\ref{sec:account} computes the sharp constant, within 5\% of a feasible design at every separation.

\subsection{A worked example: two Kalman desks}\label{sec:worked}

To make the orders concrete, consider two desks that track the same trend with Kalman filters (Lemma~\ref{lem:micro}(i)) and differ only in their signal-to-noise ratios: $\sigma_\zeta^2/\sigma_\nu^2=0.09$ and $0.16$. Their gains are $\eta_1=0.2584$ and $\eta_2=0.3279$, so $\bar\eta=0.2931$ and $\delta=0.0348$. With equal weights, the economy is observationally the split pair of Theorem~\ref{thm:quantity} around $\bar\eta$, and the response difference first appears at lag two, $d_2=-\kappa^2q\delta^2=-1.55\times10^{-4}$ (verified).

A supervisor who wants to tell this market from a single desk with gain $\bar\eta$ at 5\% error using a single pulse and the third observation needs $A=2\sigma\Phi^{-1}(0.95)/(\kappa^2q\delta^2)=212$ units of executed quantity, and, under a 0.5\% margin and 4 basis points of fees, single-pulse capital of $18{,}166$ par units. The pulse is very inefficient: the spectral lower bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:spectral}, evaluated at this pair ($\bar\eta=0.2931$, $\delta=0.0348$), gives about 77 par units for the optimal flat probe (rescaling the $\bar\eta=0.4$ constant by $\delta^{-4}$ alone would give 262, because the constant also depends on $\bar\eta$). Converting with the measured Roll noise (factor $(0.40/0.01)^2$), the optimal probe costs roughly $1.2\times10^5$ USDT and the naive pulse about $3\times10^7$ USDT. Two desks whose signal-to-noise ratios differ by almost a factor of two are therefore expensive, but not impossible, to tell apart by probing; three or more desks are much harder (Theorem~\ref{thm:kcohort}).

\subsection{What prices hide, in economic terms}\label{sec:primitives}

The worked example states the barrier in signal-to-noise ratios. That is the general picture. Write $s=\sigma_\zeta^2/\sigma_\nu^2$ for a desk's signal-to-noise ratio, so that by \eqref{eq:kgain} its memory rate is $1-\eta(s)$ with
\[
\eta(s)=\tfrac12\bigl(\sqrt{s^2+4s}-s\bigr),\qquad \eta'(s)=\tfrac12\Bigl(\frac{s+2}{\sqrt{s^2+4s}}-1\Bigr)>0 ,
\]
and $\eta$ is increasing and concave. With no inventory penalty, the first-order condition of the Remark on exponential memory gives the trading intensity $q_i=\beta_i/\lambda_{{\rm tc},i}$: what the desk expects to earn per unit of belief, over what it pays to trade.

\begin{proposition}[Identification in economic primitives]\label{prop:primitives}
Consider $K$ Kalman desks (Lemma~\ref{lem:micro}(i)) with signal-to-noise ratios $s_i\in[\underline s,\bar s]\subset(0,\infty)$ and intensities $q_i=\beta_i/\lambda_{{\rm tc},i}$, and write $c_-=\eta'(\bar s)$ and $c_+=\eta'(\underline s)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item Prices and aggregate order flow depend on the desks only through the measure
\[
\sum_iq_i\,\eta(s_i)\,\delta_{1-\eta(s_i)}.
\]
Desks are identified through their signal-to-noise ratios and intensities only; expected returns and trading costs enter only through their ratio, and two populations with the same intensity-weighted distribution of signal-to-noise ratios are observationally equivalent.
\item Fix a centre $s_c$ and let the ratios lie in $s_c+\Delta s\,[-1,1]\subset[\underline s,\bar s]$. Recovering the intensity-weighted distribution of signal-to-noise ratios to Wasserstein accuracy of order $\Delta s$, or its weights $q_i\eta(s_i)$, from $N$ noisy reset experiments with worst-case error probability below $1/4$ requires $N\ge c\,\sigma^2(\Delta s)^{-(4K-2)}$; on separated configurations, Prony's method of Theorem~\ref{prop:esprit} composed with $\eta^{-1}$ achieves this order. The constants are those of Theorems~\ref{thm:kcohort} and~\ref{prop:esprit} multiplied by powers of $c_-$ and $c_+$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{In words.} What prices reveal about Kalman-filter desks is the mix of how good their information is (their signal-to-noise ratios), weighted by how hard they trade. Expected returns and trading costs matter only through their ratio. Pinning down that mix carries the same steep cost as separating memory rates.

\begin{proof}
(i) By Lemma~\ref{lem:micro}(i) desk $i$ is a cohort with memory rate $1-\eta(s_i)$ and weight $q_i$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:merged} applies, and $\beta_i,\lambda_{{\rm tc},i}$ enter the economy only through $q_i$. (ii) On $[\underline s,\bar s]$, $\eta$ is a $C^1$ bijection onto its image with $c_-\le\eta'\le c_+$. Lower bound: take the two economies of Theorem~\ref{thm:kcohort} with centre $r_c=1-\eta(s_c)$ and rate spacing $\Delta\eta=c_-\Delta s$. By the mean value theorem their gains are images of ratios in $s_c+\Delta s\,[-1,1]$, so they are two populations of Kalman desks in the class, and the bound $N\ge c\,\sigma^2(\Delta\eta)^{-(4K-2)}=c\,c_-^{-(4K-2)}\sigma^2(\Delta s)^{-(4K-2)}$ applies to recovering their weights. Because $\eta$ is $c_+$-Lipschitz, the Wasserstein distance between the two distributions of ratios is at least $1/c_+$ times that between the two distributions of rates, which Theorem~\ref{thm:kcohort} bounds below by a constant times $\Delta\eta=c_-\Delta s$. Upper bound: ratios in $s_c+\Delta s\,[-1,1]$ with spacing at least $\gamma\Delta s$ have gains in $\eta(s_c)+c_+\Delta s\,[-1,1]$ with spacing at least $c_-\gamma\Delta s$, a class of the form required by Theorem~\ref{prop:esprit} with rescaled separation $\gamma c_-/c_+$; Prony's method recovers the rates and weights at that theorem's rate, and $\eta^{-1}$, which is $1/c_-$-Lipschitz, maps rate errors to ratio errors.
\end{proof}

The private information a supervisor would most like to know, how good each desk's information is, is exactly what prices hide. In the worked example the two desks' ratios differ by a factor of almost two, and separating them already costs hundreds of thousands of dollars of probing capital. The proposition takes intensities to vary independently of information quality, because they also reflect costs and capital. The next proposition asks what happens when the economics ties them together.

\begin{proposition}[Intensity tied to information quality]\label{prop:tied}
Let each desk's intensity be a known function of its signal-to-noise ratio, $q=\varphi(s)$ with $\varphi$ positive and $C^2$ on $[\underline s,\bar s]$ (for a mean--variance desk, $\varphi(s)$ increases with $s$), and let $n_i\ge0$ denote the mass of desks with ratio $s_i$ (a divisible population, not an integer desk count). Write $s(\eta)$ for the inverse gain map and $w(\eta)=\varphi(s(\eta))\,\eta$ for the weight per desk as a function of its gain.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{Unknown numbers of desks.} The merged measure is $\sum_in_i\,w(\eta(s_i))\,\delta_{1-\eta(s_i)}$. Every positive measure with finitely many atoms in $[1-\eta(\bar s),1-\eta(\underline s)]$ is of this form, with $n_i=m_i/w(\eta_i)$ for an atom of mass $m_i$ at $1-\eta_i$. So when the numbers of desks of each type are not known, tying intensity to information quality removes no economy, and every lower bound of Proposition~\ref{prop:primitives}(ii) holds unchanged.
\item \emph{Known numbers of desks.} If the $n_i$ are also known, the set of economies is smaller, but the single-versus-pair problem keeps its order: one type at gain $\bar\eta$ with $2n$ desks and two types at $\bar\eta\pm\delta$ with $n$ desks each have taps that differ by $n\,\delta^2\,\partial_\eta^2[w(\eta)(1-\eta)^m]_{\bar\eta}+o(\delta^2)$, so separating them needs $N\ge c\,\sigma^2\delta^{-4}$ reset experiments, the order the symmetric pair has when intensities vary freely.
\item \emph{Self-confirming configurations.} Suppose the ratio each desk perceives is the one implied by the closed-loop dynamics it trades in, through a map $T$ from configurations $(s_1,\dots,s_K)$ to perceived ratios that is continuous on a box $\prod_i[\underline s,\bar s]$ and maps it into itself. Then a configuration with $T(s)=s$ exists, and parts (i)--(ii) and Proposition~\ref{prop:primitives} apply at it.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) Desk type $i$ is a cohort with gain $\eta_i=\eta(s_i)$, memory rate $1-\eta_i$ and total weight $n_i\varphi(s_i)$, so it contributes $n_i\varphi(s_i)\eta_i=n_iw(\eta_i)$ to the atom at $1-\eta_i$. Since $w>0$, any atom mass $m_i\ge0$ is attained with $n_i=m_i/w(\eta_i)$. (ii) Write $g_m(\eta)=w(\eta)(1-\eta)^m$; the tap difference is $n[g_m(\bar\eta+\delta)+g_m(\bar\eta-\delta)-2g_m(\bar\eta)]=n\delta^2g_m''(\bar\eta)+o(\delta^2)$, with $\sum_m\sup|g_m''|^2<\infty$ on gains bounded away from zero, so the Euclidean distance between the two tap vectors is $O(n\delta^2)$ and the Kullback--Leibler divergence over $N$ experiments is $O(Nn^2\delta^4/\sigma^2)$; Le Cam's two-point argument gives the bound. (iii) is Brouwer's fixed-point theorem.
\end{proof}

So the barrier does not depend on intensities varying freely: it survives any known link between intensity and information quality as long as the supervisor does not also know how many desks of each kind there are, and even then the simplest separation problem keeps its $\delta^{-4}$ order. Whether the full $(\Delta\eta)^{-(4K-2)}$ exponent survives with known numbers of desks is open. Part~(iii) shows only that self-confirming configurations exist under continuity; it does not derive the map $T$ from a model of the desks' learning.

\subsection{Natural excitation and the cost of ambiguity}

Natural price innovations can also separate the pair with zero controlled quantity: an exact finite-sample test on independent three-step blocks needs order $\delta^{-4}$ blocks (Internet Appendix, Proposition~\ref{prop:passive0}), the same order as the passive Whittle bound of Section~\ref{sec:mixtures}. Non-identification also has a price on the quote side: when the relevant set of economies includes costlier ones, a robust dealer widens its quote, which the companion paper analyses \citep{niroula2026b}; cohort non-identification alone does not establish that the worst relevant cost exceeds the informed one.
