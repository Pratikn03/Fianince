\section{Policy implications}\label{sec:policy}

\paragraph{Pre-trade controls.} Rule 15c3-5 requires pre-trade controls that prevent orders exceeding credit or capital thresholds \citep{sec2010}. Static single-order caps and daily value-at-risk limits do not see transient dynamics. Lemma~\ref{thm:guard} identifies the quantity that a solvency-motivated control should bound: inventory times the shock envelope times the summed absolute impulse response. Peak-based or eigenvalue-based limits are either unsafe or blind in exactly the regimes where they matter. Our evidence locates where such controls matter. In the deepest crypto book the amplifying regime is rare and the statistic carries no forecasting value; in thin books it is more frequent, and six-hour estimates of the summed response forecast the next hour's adverse excursion beyond volatility forecasts and price impact (Section~\ref{sec:thin}). Our backtests also set a limit on what such a control can do with public data. A six-hour estimate of the summed response adds nothing to a volatility limit as a sizing input (Table~\ref{tab:backtest}); an operator certified from three days of data improves a volatility limit modestly, by 2.4\% in tail loss per unit of position (Table~\ref{tab:certguard}); and limits built on the estimate alone do worse than volatility in both. The guard's role is therefore a ceiling on worst-case loss under a certified operator and a correction to conventional volatility limits, not a replacement for them, and it is most relevant for books with under about \$0.4M of depth within 1\% of the mid.

\paragraph{Pricing reversion.} Proposition~\ref{prop:equil} shows that dealers under-supply reversion because its benefit, lower tail exposure for every leveraged position, is not paid to them. Maker rebates are a natural instrument: a rebate that rises with the book's summed response, $(1-\theta/N)\Lambda S^2/\tilde\kappa$ per unit of reversion, prices the externality with an observable. Venues already tier rebates by volume; tiering them by contribution to reversion in thin books is a design question our model makes precise.

\paragraph{What can be certified, what cannot, and when to abstain.} A supervisor or venue can certify the summed response, and hence a safe admission level, from controlled experiments at a cost that does not depend on how many traders there are or how similar their memories are (Theorem~\ref{thm:certcost}). It cannot certify any finite risk bound from a finite window without a bound on how long participants remember (Theorem~\ref{thm:window}(iii)), so a declared memory bound is a policy input, not a technicality, and a certificate must be renewed when conditions change (test T09). When compatible risks straddle the budget, the right response is to abstain: throttle admission to the certified level, and when even zero admission is uncertified because of existing inventory, flag the state and reduce the position (Theorem~\ref{thm:robustguard}(iv)). Disclosure is worth paying for where memory is long and public certificates are weak, and is worth little where a short window already pins the risk down (Theorem~\ref{thm:disclosure}). In dollar terms, the U.S.\ Consolidated Audit Trail central repository costs $\$248\text{ million}$ to $\$272\text{ million}$ per year across roughly $10{,}000$ NMS symbols---about $\$25{,}000$ per symbol-year (\citealp{sec2012,sec2016,sec2025}; companion paper, Section~8.2)---whereas on a single $\$1\text{M}$ $20\times$ position in a thin book (Table~\ref{tab:p21}), the certified guard saves $\$398$ to $\$2{,}557$ per hour in 95\% expected shortfall and $\$107{,}301$ to $\$1{,}171{,}114$ per year net of trading costs beyond a volatility cap, and when memory approaches the unit circle ($\bar r\to 1$) audited exposure restores up to the entire oracle trading capacity that a finite-window price certificate must forfeit (Theorem~\ref{thm:disclosure}(iii)).

\paragraph{Market design.} Frequent batch auctions remove latency races within a batch \citep{budish2015}. Transient amplification, when it exists, operates over many seconds and spans many batches, so batching and a state-carrying guard address different failures and can be complements.

\paragraph{Supervision without identification.} The companion paper shows that identifying algorithms from public data is expensive. The guard needs only impulse coefficients and operator confidence sets, which are invariant to how flow is split across cohorts. Supervisors can therefore ask venues and brokers to report summed-gain statistics and their confidence radii instead of attempting to infer black-box learners. Our results argue for reporting impact, mean reversion and refill directly, because those are the primitives that decide whether a book can amplify. They also show that feedback and amplification are different objects: measured feedback is largest in the deepest books, where amplification is rarest (Table~\ref{tab:x3}).

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The theory answers four questions. A book amplifies when depletion outweighs impact-weighted reversion; with momentum the one-period margin is sufficient but not exact, and momentum alone cannot make a stable book overshoot. Cumulative risk is long-run impact divided by reversion net of momentum, and the guard that bounds it is the largest safe pre-trade rule, computable and identified from public data. In equilibrium reversion is under-supplied, and the corrective price is a function of the guard's own statistic. And the information that certifies a safe decision is much cheaper than the information that identifies the traders: the summed response is certified at the parametric rate, finite windows certify it only under a memory bound, and a guard built on the certificate gives up no more trading capacity than any comparably safe rule, up to a logarithm.

The evidence is mixed in an informative way. The deepest crypto book sits on the stable side of the condition; flow depletes the side it hits on a fixed price grid, but not enough to produce a peak overshoot in the data we observe. In thin Binance books, six-hour estimates of cumulative transient risk forecast the size of the next hour's adverse excursion out of sample in four pre-registered tests, in unit-free form, beyond price impact and beyond a full volatility-forecasting baseline. At a given volatility forecast, hours in the top quintile of the statistic have next-hour excursions about 26 basis points larger than hours in the bottom quintile. In thin US stocks, with consolidated trades, the statistic passes a fifth pre-registered test at about a fifth of the Binance size, and appears in mid-size stocks too. On Bybit it is absent with trades alone and negligible with order-book depth, the effect is much weaker in deeper Binance books, and as a sizing input it helps only once the operator is certified from days of data, and then modestly. Two final pre-registered tests used data no earlier test touched: on new days the forecasting model, frozen at its 2023--2024 estimates, and the uncertainty-aware guard both pass; on new symbols, five thin ones, the statistic forecasts beyond a baseline that includes VPIN and the guard passes again, although its interval is wide and it does not survive a correction across all primaries. On a new venue, Coinbase spot, the registered test fails, and post hoc an order-splitting placebo carries none of the Binance signal. Three final tests sharpen why and how the effect operates: across 19 thin coins on untouched dates (Part 19), the out-of-sample $R^2$ gain of $\hat C_3$ in Binance spot is only $15.2\%$ of its perpetual-futures gain ($+0.99$ vs.\ $+6.50$ points) and within perpetuals the explained variance gain is $2.2$ times larger in the top tercile of open-interest-to-depth leverage; around three Binance fee events (Part 20), zero fees multiply volume and lower price impact $\hat\kappa$ by $18.3\%$ while leaving reversion $\hat m$ and the unit-free multiple $\widehat{S/\tilde\kappa}$ unchanged, as endogenous-depth equilibrium predicts (Proposition~\ref{prop:equil}(v)); and on a $\$1\text{M}$ $20\times$ position at matched exposure (Part 21), adding the certified guard to a volatility cap trims hourly $\mathrm{ES}_{95}$ by $\$398$ on untouched days and $\$2{,}557$ ($-22.8\%$ fewer margin breaches) on untouched symbols. Thin books are risky because their prices revert too slowly relative to the impact and momentum of the leveraged flow that moves them.

Three extensions would settle what remains open. Message-level data from a regulated market, such as LOBSTER or TAQ for US equities, would extend the US test to depth and to large stocks, show where the effect ends, and allow depletion to be measured without the recentering of band depth. Data on liquidity-provider identities would test the equilibrium's prediction that fewer dealers mean more transient risk. And longer certification windows, or certification with order-level data, would show whether the modest gain of a certified guard over volatility limits can be made large.
