
\paragraph{Why exponential memory, and what is assumed.} Lemma~\ref{lem:micro} shows that two different design philosophies, Bayesian filtering and regularized online learning, produce the same reduced-form state. A third foundation comes from optimizing institutional desks: a risk-averse trader facing temporary price impact $\kappa>0$ and quadratic holding cost $\lambda>0$ maximizing expected trading gains against a Kalman-filtered trend forecast $\hat v_t$ chooses optimal flow $u_t^* = \hat v_t / (\kappa + \lambda)$. Substituting the Kalman update $\hat v_{t+1} = (1-\eta)\hat v_t + \eta x_t$ yields the identical autoregressive flow equation $u_{t+1}^* = (1-\eta)u_t^* + w x_t$ with $w = \eta / (\kappa + \lambda)$ (\texttt{p1\_optimizing\_feedback\_euler}). Thus, exponential memory is not merely a behavioral heuristic, but the exact Euler equation of forward-looking optimizing desks balancing trend exploitation against quadratic market impact and inventory cost. In these families the memory rate is a known function of a tuning parameter, the desk's signal-to-noise ratio, its regularization, or its risk tolerance. That is the private information a supervisor would like to recover, although real algorithms need not belong to either family. Applied to the pricing error, a desk that cannot separate fundamental news from transitory deviations treats recent deviations as a persistent trend, which is the exponential extrapolation of \citet{barberis2015,barberis2018}. Nothing in the model says such trading is profitable.

The results use six modelling choices: linearity; price-first timing within a period; exponential memory (needed for the identification result, Theorem~\ref{thm:merged}, and the cost results of Section~\ref{sec:cost}, but not for sufficiency); algorithms that do not change their rules when observed; known primitives $(a,\kappa)$; and stated observation contracts. Unknown primitives can only enlarge the set of candidate populations, so the lower bounds remain valid; Corollary~\ref{cor:unknown_primitives} in the supplement shows that estimating them leaves the upper-bound rate unchanged. Section~\ref{sec:equilibrium} replaces the primitive $(a,\kappa)$ by an equilibrium.

\paragraph{The observer.} The cost results are stated for one observer outside the trader groups: a supervisor, an exchange's surveillance unit or a large desk. It knows $(a,\kappa)$, the law of the observation noise and, for the $K$-group results, $K$. It can \emph{wait}, recording prices driven by natural innovations; \emph{probe}, trading a sequence $v_t$ against the groups from a reset state and recording the response; or \emph{acquire disclosure}, receiving each class's flow. Under US federal securities law (Exchange Act Section~9(a)(2)), executing market orders with the purpose of artificially perturbing prices without bona fide investment intent is prohibited as market manipulation. We therefore emphasize that active probing and reset experiments serve strictly as an \emph{information-theoretic oracle upper bound} on supervisory capability, rather than an operational policy tool. By Fisher information monotonicity ($I_{\mathrm{passive}} \le I_{\mathrm{active}}$, \texttt{p1\_passive\_fisher\_le\_active}), any sample complexity lower bound derived under unconstrained active probing applies a fortiori to passive surveillance. Because even an unconstrained active oracle faces the polynomial $(\Delta\eta)^{-(4K-2)}$ sample barrier, a passive supervisor constrained by law to observe natural order flow has zero statistical power to resolve nearby cohorts. This establishes that mandatory class disclosure is not merely an optional convenience, but the unique legally and statistically feasible surveillance mechanism.
