\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

\section{Machines in the loop}

Markets are moving from human traders to machines. Prices that were once set by people reading news and order books are now set mostly by algorithms that read prices, trade on them in milliseconds and, increasingly, learn from the market while they trade in it. In 2026 the transition reached its next stage: banks and trading firms began to delegate decisions to AI agents. Supervisors responded in the same month from three directions. The Financial Stability Board issued sound practices for AI adoption built on governance inside each firm, risk-based triggers for human approval and oversight of shared vendors \citep{t:fsb2026}; US banking agencies asked banks how they monitor their agents and how they would shut them down \citep{t:reuters2026}; and the Bank for International Settlements warned that institutions using similar models, data or decision rules may act at the same time and amplify price moves, so that supervisors must look beyond individual institutions \citep{t:bis2026}.

All of these instruments, like the older ones, rest on seeing inside. Under MiFID~II firms must flag algorithmic orders and identify the algorithm behind each decision; the US Consolidated Audit Trail records who stands behind every order; the new guidance asks firms to document, monitor and be able to stop each agent. Behind them sits an implicit claim: that a market cannot be kept safe without knowing who is trading in it and how. Yet the risk the BIS names is not a property of any one agent. It is a property of the loop that many agents form with prices: agents trade on prices, prices move, and agents trade again.

\section{The question}

This thesis tests the implicit claim. Its central question is:
\begin{quote}
\emph{When can a market with unknown adaptive agents be certified safe without identifying the agents, and what does each kind of knowledge cost?}
\end{quote}

\section{The answer: certify, don't identify}

The information that certifies safety is far smaller and far cheaper than the information that identifies the agents.

\emph{Identifying is expensive.} Prices reveal the agents only through a merged measure of their memory rates and weights (Chapter~\ref{ch:p1}). Recovering the agents from that measure is a moment problem whose cost explodes as agents become similar: the trading capital needed to separate two memory rates $\delta$ apart grows as $\delta^{-4}$, the number of experiments needed to separate $K$ agents as $(\Delta\eta)^{-(4K-2)}$, and with general memory no method does better than accuracy $1/\log N$. Passive observation does not help.

\emph{Certifying is cheap.} The worst-case transient loss of a position depends on the agents only through one number, the summed price response, and a pre-trade guard on that number is the largest safe admission rule (Chapter~\ref{ch:p2}). The number is certified with $\sigma^2\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\epsilon)$ experiments whatever the number and spacing of the agents, and from passive data with a record of order one over the stability margin, uniformly as the margin vanishes, for the estimator used in practice (Chapter~\ref{ch:p3}).

\emph{The one thing safety needs from the agents is a memory bound.} Without one, a slow momentum agent can make a market unstable while leaving no trace in any finite record, so no passive certificate exists (Theorem~\ref{thm:hidden}). Chapter~\ref{ch:p3} explains the whole pattern: every question an observer can ask is a functional of the merged measure, the cost of a linear question is fixed exactly by a polynomial approximation problem, ``is the market safe?'' sits on the boundary of the parametric regime and pays exactly one logarithm, and ``who is trading?'' needs the fine structure of the measure and is expensive. The safety half survives agents that are nonlinear, trade several assets and keep learning, at the price of certificates that expire.

\emph{For AI agents this gives an instrument.} Agents built on one model act as one: a shared model lowers the stability margin in proportion to its adoption share and multiplies the shocks the loop amplifies (Proposition~\ref{prop:monoculture}). A supervisor can certify the margin daily from public prices and anonymous flow and trigger a market-wide pause on its trend, asking each deployer for only two numbers, a sensitivity bound and a memory bound, and for nothing about the model, its data or its strategy (Chapter~\ref{ch:ai}).

\emph{What the evidence shows.} The identification half holds in data and replicates on symbols and days never used before. The safety half holds in theory and in a limited form in data: in registered tests on new markets, public data decide the safety question where they do not identify the agents once the sample is long enough, and in thin books the safety statistic forecasts the size of adverse excursions beyond a full volatility baseline; but the decision rests mainly on price reversion, the margin does not forecast adverse moves or rank damage on stress days, and a guard estimated from public data is no better than a volatility limit. Of the 23 primary hypotheses of the thesis's own registered tests, 11 pass; of the 25 of Paper~2's program, 15 pass; every failure is reported (Chapter~\ref{ch:tests}). In a registered simulation of learning agents adopting a shared model, the margin's trend warned about two weeks before instability in 96\% of markets with no false alarm in calm ones, while the threshold computed from a model of the agents was wrong by nearly a factor of two; with real language-model desks the threshold erred in the other direction and the certificate again warned in a pilot (Chapter~\ref{ch:ai}). Thresholds built on a model of the agents can err either way; the certificate needs no such model.

\section{Contributions}

\paragraph{Identification limits (Chapter~\ref{ch:p1}).} In a linear cohort model whose reduced form arises from Kalman filtering and regularized online learning, prices identify only the merged rate--weight measure. Separating a known pair costs capital of order $\delta^{-4}$; $K$ unknown cohorts cost $(\Delta\eta)^{-(4K-2)}$ experiments, matched by Prony's method; general memory gives the logarithmic rate; passive data cost the same polynomial order. Class-tagged disclosure removes the barrier, and trader-class positions from Binance and the CFTC pin down memories that prices leave unrestricted.

\paragraph{Safety without identification (Chapter~\ref{ch:p2}).} Cumulative transient risk has the closed form $S=\tilde\kappa/(m-q\tilde\kappa)$, a book overshoots if and only if depletion outweighs impact-weighted reversion, and the summed-response guard is the largest safe admission rule. $S$ is certified at a cost independent of the cohort structure, the certified guard is minimax-optimal in lost capacity, disclosure has a computable value, and reversion is under-supplied in equilibrium. In a registered empirical program the statistic forecasts adverse excursions in thin books, while mechanism tests fail.

\paragraph{A theory of certification without identification (Chapter~\ref{ch:p3}).} Questions are functionals of the merged measure and observation contracts are noise models on its moments. The exact cost of every linear question is a modulus of continuity; a question is parametric exactly when its kernel has square-summable coefficients; questions singular at infinite memory cannot be certified at all; the safety question costs exactly $\sigma^2\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\epsilon)$. Passive data certify the margin at cost $1/M$ for any number of finite-memory cohorts, uniformly near the boundary and for the pooled estimator; without a memory bound no passive certificate exists; with contrarian agents the absolute response is certified instead. The guard stays safe for nonlinear agents whose rules, memories and weights change, given a bound on their total sensitivity, and certificates expire at a computable rate when agents learn.

\paragraph{AI agents in the loop (Chapter~\ref{ch:ai}).} The monoculture proposition, the two-number disclosure with a loop-level trigger, a registered simulation of learning agents adopting a shared model (Test~B1, all four primaries passed), and language-model trading desks in the same market: three pilots and a registered test (Test~B2) whose run is pending.

\paragraph{Registered tests (Chapter~\ref{ch:tests}).} Twelve tests of the thesis as a whole were registered, each pushed to a public repository before its data were downloaded; ten were run once, two are prospective, one on participant-level data from a venue that publishes every trade with both accounts. All results, including twelve failed primaries, are reported as they came out, and every number in the thesis is checked automatically against the file that produced it.

\section{Relation to existing work}

The thesis connects four literatures that rarely meet. \emph{System identification} \citep{t:ljung1999} asks how well a system's parameters can be recovered from input--output data; Paper~1 shows that in closed-loop markets the relevant parameters are zeros of the transfer function and are recoverable only at polynomial or logarithmic rates. \emph{Mixture and moment problems} in statistics give the closest mathematical relatives: the optimal rate for estimating a $k$-atom mixing distribution is $n^{-1/(4k-2)}$ \citep{t:heinrich2018,t:wu2020}, super-resolution has the same Vandermonde geometry \citep{t:candes2014,t:moitra2015}, and the estimation of smooth \emph{functionals} of a distribution can be far easier than estimating the distribution itself \citep{t:lepski1999,t:cai2011}. The theory of Paper~3 is the counterpart of that last observation for safety questions in feedback markets. \emph{Robust and positive control} \citep{t:megretski1997,t:farina2000,t:rantzer2015} supplies the comparison arguments of Paper~3 and the $\ell_1$ gain that bounds worst-case loss. \emph{Partial identification} in econometrics \citep{t:manski2003} provides the stance: when the structure is not identified, ask which decisions are nonetheless determined. The closest stance in economics is the \emph{sufficient-statistics} approach \citep{t:chetty2009}, in which a welfare question depends on a few reduced-form quantities rather than on the full structure. The summed response plays that role here, with two differences: it is derived from the closed-loop dynamics rather than posited, and its cost of certification is computed exactly, including the long-memory case in which it cannot be certified at all. \emph{Robust control} in economics \citep{t:hansensargent2008} asks for decisions that survive model uncertainty; the guard is such a decision, certified from data rather than from a specified set of models. In finance, the closest work is on transient impact and resilience, liquidity spirals and automated-market crashes, reviewed in Chapters~\ref{ch:p1} and~\ref{ch:p2} \citep{t:brunnermeier2009,t:kirilenko2017}. On AI agents in finance, the supervisory literature of 2026 \citep{t:fsb2026,t:bis2026} names the risk this thesis models, institutions acting alike because they share models and data, but proposes instruments that act one firm at a time; Chapter~\ref{ch:ai} supplies an instrument that acts on the loop.

\section{Reading guide}

Chapters~\ref{ch:p1} and~\ref{ch:p2} are self-contained papers with their own notation, appendices and references; Chapter~\ref{ch:p2} is condensed from the published version, keeping its theorems, proofs and every registered test. Chapter~\ref{ch:p3} states the common framework and can be read after either. Chapter~\ref{ch:ai} applies the theory to AI agents. Chapter~\ref{ch:tests} reports the registered tests of the thesis's empirical claims, failures included, and Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes with the open problems. Appendix~\ref{app:status} lists the status of every main result and maps the notation across chapters.
