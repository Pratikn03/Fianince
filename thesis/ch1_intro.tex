\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

\section{The question}

Markets are moving from human traders to machines. Where prices were once set by people reading news and order books, they are now set mostly by algorithms that read prices, trade on them in milliseconds, and increasingly learn from the market while they trade in it. This transition changes what can be known about a market and what it takes to keep it safe, and it is the subject of this thesis.

Electronic markets are feedback systems. Trading algorithms watch prices, trade on what they see, and move the prices they watch. The algorithms are private, heterogeneous and changing, and a growing share of them learn from the market while they trade in it. Whoever is responsible for safety in such a market, a regulator, an exchange or a dealer, faces agents it cannot see.

The usual response is to ask to see inside. Under MiFID~II, firms must flag algorithmic orders and identify the algorithm behind each decision; the US Consolidated Audit Trail records who stands behind every order. Behind these requirements sits an implicit claim: that a market cannot be kept safe without knowing who is trading in it.

This thesis tests that claim. Its central question is:
\begin{quote}
\emph{When can a market with unknown adaptive agents be certified safe without identifying the agents, and what does each kind of knowledge cost?}
\end{quote}

\section{The answer in one paragraph}

The information that certifies safety is far smaller and far cheaper than the information that identifies the agents. Prices reveal the agents only through a merged measure of their memory rates and weights (Chapter~\ref{ch:p1}). Recovering the agents from that measure is a moment problem whose cost explodes as agents become similar: the trading capital needed to separate two memory rates $\delta$ apart grows as $\delta^{-4}$, the number of experiments needed to separate $K$ agents as $(\Delta\eta)^{-(4K-2)}$, and with general memory no method does better than accuracy $1/\log N$. Safety needs much less. The worst-case transient loss of a position depends on the agents only through one number, the summed price response, and that number is certified with $\sigma^2\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\epsilon)$ experiments whatever the number and spacing of the agents, provided their memory is bounded (Chapter~\ref{ch:p2}). Chapter~\ref{ch:p3} explains why, and shows that this count cannot be improved: every question an observer can ask is a functional of the merged measure, and for linear questions the cost is fixed exactly by a modulus of continuity, which is the value of a polynomial approximation problem. A question is answerable at the parametric rate exactly when its kernel has square-summable power-series coefficients; ``is the market safe?'' sits on that boundary and pays exactly one logarithm; ``who is trading?'' needs the fine structure of the measure and is expensive; questions that are singular at infinite memory cannot be answered from finite data at all. The safety half survives when agents are nonlinear, trade several assets and keep learning, at the price of certificates that expire. In data the identification half holds and replicates on new days. The safety half holds in theory and, in a limited form, in data: in registered tests on two sets of new markets, public data decide the safety question where they do not identify the agents, once the sample is long enough, but the decision rests mainly on price reversion, the summed response itself is no more precise than the separation of memories, and a guard estimated from public data is no better than a volatility limit. What makes certification attainable in practice is disclosure, or an outside shock that acts as a reset experiment (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}).

\section{Three papers}

The thesis consists of three papers, framed by this introduction and a conclusion.

\paragraph{Paper 1: identification limits (Chapter~\ref{ch:p1}).} In a linear cohort model whose reduced form arises from Kalman filtering and regularized online learning, prices identify only the merged rate--weight measure. Separating a known pair costs executed quantity of order $\delta^{-2}$ and capital of order $\delta^{-4}$, with explicit constants and a finite-horizon certificate; $K$ unknown cohorts cost $(\Delta\eta)^{-(4K-2)}$ experiments, matched by Prony's method; general completely monotone memory gives the logarithmic rate; passive data cost the same polynomial order. Class-tagged disclosure removes the barrier and is worth a factor $(\Delta\eta)^{-(4K-4)}$ in samples. Trader-class positions from Binance and the CFTC pin down memories that prices leave unrestricted.

\paragraph{Paper 2: safety without identification (Chapter~\ref{ch:p2}).} In a limit order book with adaptive cohorts, cumulative transient risk has the closed form $S=\tilde\kappa/(m-q\tilde\kappa)$, a book overshoots if and only if depletion outweighs impact-weighted reversion, and a pre-trade guard on the summed impulse response is the largest safe admission rule. $S$ is certified with $O(\sigma^2\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\epsilon))$ experiments; a finite window certifies it only under a memory bound; the certified guard is minimax-optimal in lost capacity up to a logarithmic factor; disclosure has a computable value for safe capacity; and reversion is under-supplied in equilibrium. In a pre-registered empirical program the statistic forecasts adverse excursions in thin books, while mechanism tests fail.

\paragraph{Paper 3: a theory of certification without identification (Chapter~\ref{ch:p3}).} Questions are treated as functionals of the merged measure and observation contracts as noise models on its moments. For linear questions the paper determines the cost of certification up to a constant factor in accuracy by a modulus of continuity and its dual, a polynomial approximation problem with penalized coefficients; shows that a question is parametric exactly when its kernel has square-summable coefficients; and shows that questions singular at infinite memory cannot be certified at all (Theorem~\ref{thm:dich} and Proposition~\ref{prop:parametric}, \Proved{}). For the safety question the cost is exactly $\sigma^2\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\epsilon)$, with explicit constants: the logarithm in Paper~2's certificate cannot be removed (Theorem~\ref{thm:philog}, \Proved{}). Questions that depend on finitely many responses are parametric, and the peak response, the question an operator might ask first, is known without data and carries no information about loss (Corollary~\ref{cor:peak}, \Proved{}). The rates of Papers 1 and 2 appear as cases of one picture. A robust safety bound then shows that the summed-gain guard stays safe for nonlinear agents whose rules, memories and weights change over time, given only a bound on their total sensitivity (Theorem~\ref{thm:sector}, \Proved{}), with a sharp version for several assets with positive cross-impact (Proposition~\ref{prop:multiasset_positive}, \Proved{}), and a corollary gives the lifetime of a certificate when agents keep learning (Corollary~\ref{thm:lifetime}, \Proved{}); in a registered simulation with profit-driven learners the lifetime is close to tight when trend-following pays. Whether the guard survives agents who adapt to it is open: a discontinuous guard can leave the agents' game without an equilibrium.

\section{Relation to existing work}

The thesis connects four literatures that rarely meet. \emph{System identification} \citep{t:ljung1999} asks how well a system's parameters can be recovered from input--output data; Paper~1 shows that in closed-loop markets the relevant parameters are zeros of the transfer function and are recoverable only at polynomial or logarithmic rates. \emph{Mixture and moment problems} in statistics give the closest mathematical relatives: the optimal rate for estimating a $k$-atom mixing distribution is $n^{-1/(4k-2)}$ \citep{t:heinrich2018,t:wu2020}, super-resolution has the same Vandermonde geometry \citep{t:candes2014,t:moitra2015}, and the estimation of smooth \emph{functionals} of a distribution can be far easier than estimating the distribution itself \citep{t:lepski1999,t:cai2011}. The theory of Paper~3 is the counterpart of that last observation for safety questions in feedback markets. \emph{Robust and positive control} \citep{t:megretski1997,t:farina2000,t:rantzer2015} supplies the comparison arguments of Paper~3 and the $\ell_1$ gain that bounds worst-case loss. \emph{Partial identification} in econometrics \citep{t:manski2003} provides the stance: when the structure is not identified, ask which decisions are nonetheless determined. The closest stance in economics is the \emph{sufficient-statistics} approach \citep{t:chetty2009}, in which a welfare question depends on a few reduced-form quantities rather than on the full structure. The summed response plays that role here, with two differences: it is derived from the closed-loop dynamics rather than posited, and its cost of certification is computed exactly, including the long-memory case in which it cannot be certified at all. \emph{Robust control} in economics \citep{t:hansensargent2008} asks for decisions that survive model uncertainty; the guard is such a decision, certified from data rather than from a specified set of models. In finance, the closest work is on transient impact and resilience, liquidity spirals and automated-market crashes, reviewed in Chapters~\ref{ch:p1} and~\ref{ch:p2} \citep{t:brunnermeier2009,t:kirilenko2017}.

\section{Reading guide}

Chapters~\ref{ch:p1} and~\ref{ch:p2} are self-contained papers and keep their own notation, appendices and references. Chapter~\ref{ch:p3} can be read after either of them: it states the common framework, and each of its results points to the chapter where the special case is proved. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} reports the registered tests of the thesis's empirical claims, including those that failed, and the open problems. Appendix~\ref{app:status} lists the status of every main result and maps the notation across chapters.
