\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
Electronic markets are feedback systems in which many adaptive trading algorithms respond to prices that their own trades move. Regulators often respond by asking to know which algorithms are trading. This thesis asks whether that knowledge is needed for safety, and answers that it generally is not. In the closed-loop markets studied here, prices reveal the agents only through a merged measure of their memories and weights; separating two agents whose memory rates differ by $\delta$ costs trading capital of order $\delta^{-4}$, separating $K$ unknown agents costs a number of experiments of order $(\Delta\eta)^{-(4K-2)}$, and with general memory the accuracy of any method improves only as $1/\log N$. Safety, by contrast, depends on the agents only through one number, the summed price response, which is certified with $\sigma^2\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\epsilon)$ experiments whatever their number and spacing, provided memory is bounded, and cannot be certified at all without such a bound; the logarithm cannot be removed. A pre-trade guard built on the certificate is the largest safe admission rule and is minimax-optimal in lost capacity up to a logarithmic factor. A third paper organises these results under one framework in which every question an observer can ask is a functional of the agents' merged measure. For linear questions it determines the cost of certification exactly, up to a constant factor in accuracy, as the value of a polynomial approximation problem with penalized coefficients: a question is answerable at the parametric rate exactly when its kernel has square-summable coefficients, and the safety question sits on that boundary and pays exactly one logarithm. It extends the safety results to sector-bounded nonlinear agents, to several assets and to agents that keep learning, for which a certificate has a computable lifetime. In data, trader-class disclosures from Binance and the CFTC restrict a persistence that prices leave unrestricted, and in pre-registered tests the summed-response statistic forecasts adverse price excursions in thin crypto books and, more weakly, in thin US stocks; tests of a leverage or liquidation mechanism fail. Ten further registered tests, each run once, replicate the disclosure result on new days and on new symbols, and on three sets of markets never used before decide the safety question where the agents are not identified: with 430 days per market the margin is decided in 23 of 25 and in 24 of 26 markets, all safe, while the memories are identified in at most one. The decision rests mainly on price reversion; the summed response itself is no more precise than the separation of memories; a guard estimated from public data does not reduce realized tail losses; the estimated margin does not forecast the next day's largest adverse move or extreme moves, and does not rank damage on market-wide stress days, but on 34 new markets it forecasts next-day variance beyond the Hawkes branching ratio, as registered, and on 36 more the size of the estimated closed-loop response scales with next-day losses at about half the predicted rate; the class persistence that disclosure reveals is, once corrected for bias, close to a random walk within the day; and an overshoot seen in a pilot after scheduled macro releases does not replicate on earlier releases. Eleven of their twenty-three primary hypotheses pass. The evidence supports the thesis in a narrower form than the theory: prices do not identify the agents, and with enough data they still answer the safety question for markets far from the boundary; nearer the boundary, disclosure or an outside shock that acts as an experiment is needed.
