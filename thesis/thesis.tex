\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
Electronic markets are feedback systems in which many adaptive trading algorithms respond to prices that their own trades move. Regulators often respond by asking to know which algorithms are trading. This thesis asks whether that knowledge is needed for safety, and answers that it generally is not. In the closed-loop markets studied here, prices reveal the agents only through a merged measure of their memories and weights; separating two agents whose memory rates differ by $\delta$ costs trading capital of order $\delta^{-4}$, separating $K$ unknown agents costs a number of experiments of order $(\Delta\eta)^{-(4K-2)}$, and with general memory the accuracy of any method improves only as $1/\log N$. Safety, by contrast, depends on the agents only through one number, the summed price response, which is certified at the parametric rate $\sigma^2\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\epsilon)$ whatever their number and spacing, provided memory is bounded, and cannot be certified at all without such a bound. A pre-trade guard built on the certificate is the largest safe admission rule and is minimax-optimal in lost capacity up to a logarithmic factor. A third paper organises these results under one framework in which every question an observer can ask is a functional of the agents' merged measure, and its cost is set by how the functional behaves as memory becomes long. It extends the safety results to sector-bounded nonlinear agents and to agents that keep learning, for which a certificate has a computable lifetime. In data, trader-class disclosures from Binance and the CFTC pin down memories that prices leave unrestricted, and in pre-registered tests the summed-response statistic forecasts adverse price excursions in thin crypto books and, more weakly, in thin US stocks; tests of a leverage or liquidation mechanism fail.
